The completed 72-game validation largely confirms the pilot: payoff-based action choice and coordination transfer across held-out families, while mutual cooperation transfers poorly across families. Known-family cooperation and coordination are already captured well by a family-rate baseline, and model IDs add little. The action-choice gain includes basic mapping from arbitrary encoded actions to payoff roles; it does not establish nuanced strategic modeling. The explicitly post-pilot payoff-dominant equilibrium comparator remains stronger on family-held-out Brier in the expanded dataset, materially weakening claims based only on beating the original Nash selector.

The family-held-out action result is the clearest pilot success: combined logistic reduces Brier from 0.2527 to 0.1718, a paired improvement of **0.0809 [0.0502, 0.1140]**. The derived-only logistic is slightly better at 0.1697, so raw payoff coordinates and model IDs are not necessary for that aggregate result. Coordination improves from 0.2230 to 0.1615, a gain of **0.0615 [0.0515, 0.0709]**; the combined MLP reaches 0.1553. These are descriptive 95% game/episode bootstrap intervals, conditional on the fitted forecasts and unadjusted across the many inspected contrasts.

There are only three or four games in each held family, and success is not uniform. For action probability, combined logistic remains near 0.25 in anti-coordination, chicken and coordination games. It performs much better in harmony (0.0399), weak dominance (0.0756) and stag hunt (0.1330); stationary Nash already scores 0 and 0.0167 in the first two. Much of the learned improvement beyond the original Nash selector comes from stag hunt and coordination rather than a universal gain across every strategic regime; the secondary payoff-dominant rule directly addresses this selection difference.

Cooperation illustrates the generalization gap. Random-group combined logistic scores **0.0748**, essentially matching the simple known-family baseline at **0.0735**; family holdout increases its error to **0.3096**. The family-held-out cooperation improvement over Pair is only 0.0209 [−0.0664, 0.1008]. The MLP does improve over Pair by 0.0694 [0.0345, 0.0991], but its Brier remains 0.2611 and its aggregate predicted/observed correlation is −0.297. The **original Nash selector** does not account for all action and coordination signal: the family-held-out paired increments are positive, although the action interval only narrowly excludes zero. The original rule often uses the symmetric interior mixed equilibrium when multiple pure equilibria exist; its conditional-target forecasts are unsupported.

Calibration reinforces the limits of the pilot. Family action combined-logistic ECE is 0.1506 (derived-only 0.1060), despite improved Brier; corresponding log loss is 0.5247 versus Pair 0.6986 and Nash 0.9572. A nearly constant marginal/pair predictor can have lower ECE while lacking useful discrimination. Random-group cooperation has ECE 0.0222 and rate MAE 0.1018 for combined logistic, but family cooperation has ECE 0.2444, MAE 0.4027 and log loss 1.0867. Extrapolated coordination is badly calibrated: combined ECE **0.4634**, versus Nash **0.0484**. The model-holdout broad-target ECEs are much smaller (0.0380 action, 0.0494 cooperation, 0.0384 coordination), consistent with strong known-shape transfer. ECE uses ten fixed bins; bin-boundary changes can make nearly equal predictors have different ECE, so it is not a stand-alone ranking criterion. The saved bins show the direction of the errors: family action predictions are often too close to 0.5 (a bin averaging 0.281 predicts an observed 0.011 rate, while a 0.636 bin observes 0.901). Family cooperation has a high-confidence bin predicting 0.959 but observing 0.376. Extrapolated coordination puts every scored row in the highest bin, predicting **0.977** against an observed **0.514**. These are concrete calibration failures, not just differences in a scalar summary.

First-action probability is a separate one-opportunity target. Combined logistic scores 0.1512 on family holdout, versus Pair 0.2549 and Nash 0.2304. Since the player has no interaction history at that point, some predictive payoff structure is already present before retaliation or adaptation can occur. This does not support attributing the repeated-action prediction gain to learning within the episode.

Conditional findings are materially weaker. In the full pilot common support, retaliation has 15 eligible games, 631 focal records and 2,824 opportunities; forgiveness has **11 games, 253 focal records and only 295 opportunities**; exploitation has eight games, 532 focal records and 1,842 opportunities. Retaliation improves in random-group and known-shape pair/model tests but does not transfer cleanly by family (combined Brier 0.3226 versus Pair 0.3511 and marginal 0.3134). Forgiveness deteriorates under family holdout (combined 0.3603 versus Pair 0.2616) and random-group performance is near baseline (0.2300 versus 0.2270). Exploitation results change direction across splits and methods: combined is worse than Pair in family/random/model tests, with small gains elsewhere. These do not establish a reliable, general predictor of forgiveness or exploitation.

Conditional Brier gains need an additional weighting qualification. Marginal/Pair baselines average eligible row rates equally within each canonical game; logistic fitting and event Brier weight opportunities within each game. Gains can therefore partly reflect estimating an event-weighted average more closely, in addition to learning predictive structure. Conditional opportunity sets themselves depend on the observed trajectories. The constant-eight-opportunity action/cooperation/coordination targets avoid this particular denominator mismatch; first action has a constant denominator of one. Missing conditional opportunities remain undefined, not zero. Retaliation, forgiveness and exploitation are the protocol's descriptive event patterns, not causal mechanisms or mental-state labels.
