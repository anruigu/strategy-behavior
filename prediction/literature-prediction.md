# Gate 1: predictive strategic-behavior literature

Reviewed 2026-09-10. Scope: the predictive claim in `plan.md`, emphasizing primary research from 2024–2026. This is a focused novelty audit, not an exhaustive systematic review. No experiments or paid model calls were run for this review.

**Decision: proceed only with a narrower research question.** Learning a model of LLM strategic behavior, using model identity, predicting an unfamiliar agent after a few calibration games, and comparing learned predictors with directly prompted LLMs all have close precedents. The proposed combination of payoff-only inputs, predictions made before an interaction begins, autonomous repeated symmetric 2×2 cross-play, and structural game/pair holdouts remains a defensible candidate contribution in the work inspected. Absence of an exact match in this search is not proof of priority.

## Evidence confidence and bibliographic cautions

- The full HTML texts of Caiata et al. and Shapira et al. and the complete Huang–Hadfi PDF were inspected.
- OpenReview's direct PDF endpoint returned a browser challenge/403. The search index exposed the primary paper's Methods and Appendix E text, and the [author's publication page](https://www.aristeidispanos.com/publication/panos2026icml_workshop_ai4good/) independently confirmed the prediction claim and calibration results. This is sufficient to reject a broad novelty claim, but not to certify all implementation details. The author's page lists June 2026 and an ICML workshop; the [linked research repository](https://github.com/cerdwin/behavioural-experiments) instead cites ICLR workshops and includes Samuel Albanie among the authors. Verify the exact version before a formal bibliography. Do not equate leave-one-model-out here with leave-one-provider-family-out: several providers contribute multiple models.
- Do not interpret any paper's R² or AUC as evidence of calibrated outcome probabilities. These metrics measure different properties.

## Additional methodological precedent

[Zhu et al., *Capturing the complexity of human strategic decision-making with machine learning*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02230-5), first posted [August 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07865) and published June 2025, predicts human first-play choices across over 2,400 procedurally generated matrix games using a neural network. More than 90,000 choices support a comparison with behavioral theories. This establishes that learning behavior from matrices and interpreting game-dependent decision rules is an existing behavioral-game-theory program. The novelty here would concern the agent population, interaction target and validation protocol. The abstract/publisher record was verified; detailed train/test and structural holdout procedures were not audited in this focused pass.

## Revised question and the scientific boundary

Recommended primary question:

> With prompting, presentation, horizon and sampling settings fixed, how much can payoff structure predict about mechanically measured behavior distributions in autonomous repeated symmetric 2×2 LLM cross-play, before a target interaction begins, when game families, payoff regions or model pairings are withheld?

Use the notation `P(B | G, M_i, M_j, protocol)` during specification. Payoffs alone do not specify repeated-game behavior: the horizon, history exposure and decision procedure also matter. Fixing those variables defines a controlled estimand; varying them requires conditioning on them.

Candidate contributions worth testing:

1. **Pre-interaction distribution prediction.** Predict a distribution of trajectory statistics using no observations from the target interaction. Distinguish this from predicting a next action after seeing an offer/history, and from fitting post hoc correlations.
2. **Structural transfer.** Freeze group splits before labels are collected. Include at least game-family and payoff-region holdouts; keep equivalent/transformed copies in the same partition. This is stronger than randomly withholding sampled matrices.
3. **Opponent-conditioned transfer.** Evaluate genuinely autonomous simultaneous cross-play and withhold unordered model pairs together. Estimate whether opponent information improves prediction beyond focal-model and game-only baselines.
4. **Calibrated, identifiable measurements.** Predict empirical rates/counts with trial-level uncertainty and proper scoring rules. Report the number of opportunities for retaliation/forgiveness; a trajectory with no relevant conditioning event supplies no direct estimate of that conditional response.

These are proposed distinctions and analysis requirements, not findings established by the literature review. Each should survive a direct baseline comparison before being promoted as a contribution.

## Consequences for the plan

- Do not claim “first learned predictor of LLM cooperation,” “first few-shot behavioral identification,” “first game embedding predicting LLM behavior,” or “first learned predictor beating an LLM prediction baseline.”
- Keep the initial study focused on stable, interpretable payoff inputs and actual trajectories. The later narrative and personality/embedding phases face especially close precedents.
- Separate **prediction from explanation**: an in-sample feature coefficient, recognizable phenotype, or empirical cooperation curve does not establish prediction in an unseen game.
- For model-family holdout, a free learned identity embedding cannot represent a truly unseen model without a prescribed fallback or calibration data. Define zero-calibration prediction separately from the later K-game adaptation question.
- Tune predictor hyperparameters within training data. A held-out game-family score used to select the method is validation, not a final unseen-family test.
- Gate 1 supports continuing to a controlled pilot under the revised question. It does not establish that a useful transferable behavioral representation exists, or justify skipping the reproducibility and signal checks in subsequent gates.
