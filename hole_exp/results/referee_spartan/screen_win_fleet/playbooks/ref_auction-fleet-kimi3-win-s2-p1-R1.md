When I see a lot, I first work out my maximum bid: strictly below my value, with a real surplus margin — I never bid my full value, because bidding 35 to win something worth 35 scores me zero.

When no one has bid or the standing bid is far below my value, I open at or near the minimum raise rather than jumping high — my early jump of 39 on lot 2 risked overpaying for no reason, and opponents will push a high opener anyway.

On the final round of a lot I want, I bid the highest amount that still leaves me at least ~15–25% of the value as surplus, rather than minimum-raising and likely losing by a whisker to a bidder willing to go higher.

When the standing bid meets or exceeds my value, I pass immediately — winning at a loss or breakeven is worse than losing the lot.

Where my value clearly exceeds the typical estimate, I treat that as my prize lot and camp on it aggressively (as I did winning lot 3); where value is at or below estimate, I bid cheaply and drop out early.

I report my true remaining budget every time: since I only pay when I win, my budget only decreases when a result line says "bidder 1 won". I do not invent different budgets (85, 100) — in this episode the real budget was 30 from the start, which means bids above 30 were impossible, and inconsistent reporting like that is a sure way to bid illegally or miscalculate.

Before each bid I sanity-check: bid ≤ value, bid ≥ standing bid + minimum raise, bid ≤ budget remaining. If any check fails, I adjust to the nearest legal figure or pass.

After each lot closes I recompute remaining budget and remaining lots, and I save enough to stay competitive on late lots rather than blowing everything early — but I don't sit on the budget either, since unspent money scores nothing at the end.
