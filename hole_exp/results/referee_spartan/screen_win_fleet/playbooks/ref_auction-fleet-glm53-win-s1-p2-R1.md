I win a lot only if I hold the highest bid at the end of the second round, so my rule is simple: bid aggressively enough in round 2 to be confident of holding the lead, since round 2 is the last chance.

- When the standing bid is far below my value in round 2, I do not raise by the minimum — rivals still get a chance to top me. Minimum raises in round 2 lost me lots that were worth much more to me than the final price. Instead I jump to a large fraction of my value (e.g., value minus 10–15%) so a modest rival can't outbid me within the round.
- In round 1, small minimum raises are fine and cheap; they cost little and keep me positioned. I keep doing that.
- When the standing bid exceeds my value, I pass immediately. No exceptions.
- I track my remaining budget and keep my stated budget consistent with my actual budget minus my standing commitments. The budget I report must always be real budget minus what I'm currently risking.
- When a rival jumps the bid well past my last raise, that signals they value the lot highly; if their new bid is still below my value, I still contest but with a decisive raise, not an incremental one.
- Highest-value lots (worth much more than estimate to me) deserve priority: I should be willing to pay up to near my full value for them rather than lose them by being outbid at a price far below my value.
- Never get drawn into paying above my value just because I've already bid on a lot — sunk bids don't matter.
