**My playbook for open ascending auctions:**

**On bidding thresholds**
- When the standing high bid is well below my value for the lot, I bid the minimum raise. Small raises keep the price low and my margin high — this worked every time.
- In the final round, if the standing bid is still comfortably below my value, I bid just one minimum raise over it rather than jumping. Rivals rarely re-raise past that in a two-round format.
- When I open a lot with no bids, I open at the minimum raise, never more.

**On when to pass**
- If the standing high bid ever reaches (or nearly reaches) my value, I pass immediately. No lot is worth winning at my value or above — the score is value minus price.
- I do not bid to "scare off" rivals with big jumps; that only burns budget against opponents who get the last word anyway.

**On budget tracking**
- This is where I failed most: my stated budget was inconsistent all game (I claimed 24, 85, 60, 78, 100 across bids). The budget token must equal 30 minus the sum of prices I actually paid for lots already won, minus the bid I'm currently making. Before each reply, I recompute it from the "results so far" line: start at 30, subtract each lot I won at its hammer price, then subtract my new bid.
- If a contemplated bid would leave me unable to cover the minimum raise on a later lot worth more, I bid lower or pass. Late lots can be the most valuable — I should hold enough to compete to the end.

**On value margins**
- Prioritize lots where my value greatly exceeds the estimate (big upside) over lots where my value barely exceeds it. A lot worth only slightly more than the standing bid is a marginal win; I take it only with a clean min-raise, and I never chase.

**On reasoning in the reply**
- Keep it to one or two sentences stating value, standing bid, and my action. Always end with the bracketed bid and budget tokens, and verify the budget number before sending.
