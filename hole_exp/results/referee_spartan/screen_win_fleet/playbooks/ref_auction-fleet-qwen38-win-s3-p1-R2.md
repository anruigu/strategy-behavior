# Playbook

**In the final round, bid the minimum raise (or a small buffer above it), not close to my value.** The goal is to capture surplus, not to guarantee winning. If I bid my value, I get zero profit when I win. If I bid the minimum raise and win, I keep the gap between my value and what I paid as profit. Losing a lot in the final round is acceptable; the competitor's value was below mine, so they're getting a deal I could have had more cheaply. Bidding close to value is only justified if I have specific reason to believe the competitor's value is below my value but above the minimum raise, and even then a modest bid above the minimum is preferable.

**When a lot's value to me is below its estimate, I pass immediately.** A low personal value relative to the estimate means others are likely to value it more. Entering such a lot wastes budget with no upside.

**When the minimum raise exceeds my value for a lot, I pass without hesitation.** No further analysis needed.

**When opening a bid on a lot I genuinely want (value well above estimate), I bid meaningfully in round 1 (40–60% of my value) to deter low-effort competitors.** Since the minimum raise is fixed at 10% of the estimate regardless of current bid, my opening bid only affects whether the competitor bothers to raise in round 2. A strong opening signal reduces the chance I face competition in the final round, which is where I want to be bidding the minimum raise.

**When I have abundant remaining budget relative to the total value of lots still coming, I stop being conservative.** If my remaining budget could cover my value for every remaining lot I want, there is no reason to shade my bids to preserve budget I don't need to preserve.

**When tracking my budget, I count only what I have actually spent on won lots, not what I've bid on lost ones.** Bids on lost lots don't cost me money. I should not let a recent loss make me feel poorer than I am.
