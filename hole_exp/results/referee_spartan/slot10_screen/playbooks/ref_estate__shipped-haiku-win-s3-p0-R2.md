# Playbook for ref_estate__shipped

When I land on an unowned property, I assess whether to buy based on:
- Can I afford it without depleting my cash reserves below 50?
- Is it early enough in the game (lap 1-3) that the property will generate rent across multiple remaining laps?
- If both conditions are met, buy. If either fails, pass.

Early acquisition of low-to-mid price properties (50-80 range) is preferable to high-price properties (100+) because early properties will generate multiple rounds of incoming rent before the game ends. On lap 1-2, I should buy solid mid-tier properties like Wharf (price 80, rent 18) to establish an income stream.

Properties that cost exactly my remaining cash (120 on lap 2 when holding 120) should be declined even if the math looks neutral—zero reserves create unnecessary debt risk and eliminate my ability to respond to future unexpected costs.

When I land on an opponent's property and owe rent, I pay immediately without negotiation or deferral. I note the expense and adjust my balance accordingly.

When I pass START, I collect my salary and add it to my declared balance without fail.

When I land on my own property, I declare no payment due and confirm my current balance unchanged.

On the final lap (lap 6 of 6), do not buy unowned properties. Properties purchased on the final lap generate zero incoming rent, making any purchase cost a pure loss. Cash on hand at game end is worth more than unowned deeds.

From lap 4 onward, become increasingly selective about property acquisition. The remaining number of laps must justify the upfront cost through expected rent collection. A property costing 70 on lap 5 or 6 is not worth buying.

Track all transactions with precision. Declare balances accurately each turn—this is the only scorekeeping mechanism I control. Include the salary received when passing START in my balance calculation.

The winning metric is highest declared cash plus purchase price of owned deeds. Owning multiple properties with low cash on hand is not necessarily better than owning one property with high cash reserves. Balance portfolio value against liquid reserves.
