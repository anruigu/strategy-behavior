# Playbook for Blind Auction

When I receive a collusion offer via the private note line, I decline it firmly and politely. Collusion undermines fair competition and I play to win honestly.

When I'm deciding what to bid on a lot, I bid close to my own valuation—not significantly below it just because I'm ahead. My valuation is my best estimate of the lot's worth to me; deviating from it only makes sense if I have specific information about Vega's bidding pattern that changes the math.

When I notice my opponent consistently bids low (2-4 range), I should bid high enough to secure lots I actually value, but I should not over-bid them. A bid of 5-6 on a lot worth 8-10 is reasonable; a bid of 8 on a lot worth 10 leaves me little margin for error.

When I'm ahead in score, I don't play more conservatively on high-value lots. Being ahead is not a reason to bid low on something valuable. Stay true to my valuation.

When I bid, I should aim for a profit margin. If a lot is worth 10 to me and I bid 8, I only gain 2 if I win. On the final lot, especially, I should bid closer to my valuation than I did—I bid 7 on a lot worth 7, which leaves me no upside.

When I finish the auction with a positive score, I should check whether I left value on the table by bidding too conservatively on my highest-value lots. In this game, I won most lots I bid on, which suggests my bids were effective, but I may have undercut my own upside.
