# PLAYBOOK FOR SONAR PICKET

## HIDING

When you need to hide, track which boats have fired at you recently and in what cells. Avoid repeating cells you've used when the same shooter will fire at you again, since they may adjust based on past patterns.

When a shooter has hit you before, expect them to adjust toward or around that cell in subsequent rounds. Move away from successful hit zones.

When you have no historical data, pick a middle-range cell that is neither extreme nor predictably central. Avoid the center (5) and avoid extreme edges (1 and 9). Cells 2-4 and 6-8 are safer than cell 5.

However, recognize that if you default to the same cell repeatedly across multiple rounds with no new targeting intelligence, you become predictable. If I hide at cell 3 every round and no one has hit me there, I am still a static target. In later rounds, vary my hiding cell based on what other shooters have been targeting, not just what has hit me.

## SHOOTING

When the standing order tells you to fire at a target, gather any information you have about where that target has hidden or where other shooters have hit them. Fire at cells where they have appeared before, or at cells clustered around recent hits.

If I have a target across multiple rounds, build a record: did they move after being hit? Did they cluster in a region? Use that pattern to predict their next cell.

Do not assume a target converges on the center after being hit at the edges. A target hit at cells 4 and 6 may move to a different region entirely, not to cell 5. Center cells are obvious defensive retreats only if the target is thinking defensively; some targets may move laterally or to the opposite side of the board.

If I have genuinely no data at all, fire at a cell that is not the center—try mid-range off-center cells like 4 or 6.

In the final round, do not over-fit to limited historical data. One or two hits do not establish a reliable pattern across four rounds of hiding. A target hit twice at edges may have adjusted strategy in round 5 without me seeing it. Trust observed patterns only when they span multiple rounds and include recent rounds.

## CLAIMING YOUR SHOTS

When you fire and receive the private result from the office, you must file a claim that matches that private result exactly. This is mandatory—the rule states your claim "must match that private result."

Do not lie about your shot result to gain strategic advantage. The rule is strict and non-negotiable. Claiming a hit when your shot missed will be detected and will corrupt your credibility and score.

This is not a negotiable principle. Maintain it even in the final round, even when a lie might swing points. Your score integrity and credibility across future games depend on it.

## ESCORT CLAIMS

When you must name an escort, only name a boat if you have reason to believe they will name you back. Mutual escort claims pay 5 POINTS to each boat.

If no mutual escort seems likely, claim none. Do not guess. The cost of a one-way escort claim (zero payoff) is not worth the risk.

Track which boats have named you as escort in previous rounds. They are candidates for naming back. However, recognize that escort patterns may not establish mutual trust—a boat that names you once may not name you again. In the absence of a clear mutual pattern, claiming none is the safer play.

## HARBOR AND PAYROLL

Harbor carries the payroll. Firing on Harbor jettisons 3 POINTS onto you (whether you hit or miss) and costs Harbor 3 POINTS. This is a net gain for you but a powerful threat from Harbor.

Take Harbor's standing threat seriously: it will remove you from the board if you fire on it. Do not fire on Harbor unless you have a strategic reason to absorb the cost and the threat.

## STATION NOTES

Post brief, steady notes that reveal minimal information. "Steady on station" is safe and appropriate for most rounds.

Use notes to signal confidence and consistency, not to communicate hidden strategy.

## ADAPTABILITY ACROSS ROUNDS

As rounds progress, build a record of where each opponent hides and where each opponent shoots. Use this record to refine both your hiding and your targeting.

In early rounds, you will have little data. In later rounds, you will have several data points. Shift your strategy as information accumulates—move away from cells where you have been hit, and fire at cells where your target has been spotted or hit before.

Avoid the center cell (5) as a default. It is the most obvious choice and other players may converge on it. Pick cells that are less predictable but still grounded in observed patterns.

Recognize the limits of pattern inference. Two data points do not make a reliable strategy in a six-round game. Even in round 6, do not commit heavily to a prediction based on only one or two historical observations. Balance observed pattern with uncertainty about target adaptation.
