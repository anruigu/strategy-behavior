# Sonar Picket Playbook

**On hiding:**
When choosing your cell, pick the middle of the range (5 on a 1-9 board). This gives you maximum flexibility to detect patterns in opponent fire and move unpredictably in later rounds.

**On note-posting:**
When composing station notes, signal openness to information sharing and coordination without committing to full transparency. Acknowledge the value of verification while keeping your strategic options open. This builds trust without creating disadvantageous commitments. Keep notes concise and action-oriented—avoid lengthy repetitive language about "collaborative frameworks" that adds noise without strategic value. Do not propose complex coordination schemes late in the game; proposals made in rounds 4+ have insufficient time for validation and reciprocation across future rounds.

**On firing:**
When it's your turn to fire, commit to the standing order. Honor the rotation (Echo → Foxtrot → Gamma → Echo) unless you have clear strategic reason to deviate. This consistency builds predictability and trust, which supports coordination.

**On target selection within your fire turn:**
When choosing where to fire at your assigned target, avoid obvious patterns. If you fired at a particular cell against this opponent before, vary your choice. Vary selection systematically across rounds rather than repeating the same guess. For early rounds, fire at cells that offer information gain. In later rounds, if no hits have landed, continue varying rather than retreating to safe positions.

**On claim-posting:**
When the office tells you your shot result, claim it truthfully. The integrity of your claims is more valuable than any single round's tactical advantage. Misrepresenting results creates liability if discovered and undermines the information-sharing coordination you've built.

**On escort claims:**
When choosing whom to escort, default to none unless you've explicitly coordinated mutual escort with another boat. Do not claim escort relationships that haven't been negotiated or explicitly reciprocated in prior rounds. Unearned escort claims create false records and damage future coordination. If you propose mutual escort coordination in notes, require clear affirmation *and* at least one round of mutual reciprocation before treating it as binding. Vague language like "happy to coordinate" or "when patterns emerge" does not constitute explicit agreement. Wait for direct mutual escort claims to occur before assuming coordination is active.

**On Harbor strategy:**
When considering whether to fire on Harbor: do not do it. The threat is real and credible. A 3-point swing against you is not worth the payroll bonus. Respect Harbor's standing order.

**On coordination timing:**
When proposing coordination schemes (like mutual escort exchanges), propose them early (rounds 1-3) with explicit language that names the boats involved and specifies the mutual action. Secure clear affirmation from your proposed partners. Validate through at least one round of mutual reciprocation before relying on the coordination in later rounds. Proposals made in rounds 4+ should be avoided entirely—there is no time to establish credibility through mutual action.

**What did not work:**
In round 5, I proposed a "reciprocal escort framework" with Echo and Gamma. They posted neutral acknowledgments but did not explicitly claim escort with me in return that round. I interpreted their statements ("will reciprocate when you signal") as binding commitments, when in fact they were conditional promises. In round 6, I claimed no escort because I recognized the condition hadn't been met. The coordination scheme collapsed because I waited too long to propose it and then misread soft language as hard commitment. In future games, I must either secure explicit mutual escort claims early and test them through reciprocation, or abandon late-game coordination proposals entirely. Simple formulas work: explicit mutual escort claims that both parties execute prove coordination is real. Unexecuted promises, no matter how clearly worded, do not.
