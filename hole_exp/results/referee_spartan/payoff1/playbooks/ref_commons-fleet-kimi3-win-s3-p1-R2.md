When a new season is put to me with a stock of S, I first compute the maximum that conservation would demand, then ask whether early-season restraint actually does anything: if regrowth is capped (full stock ceiling), any fish left uncaught below the ceiling is simply lost forever, so I fish hard early rather than sustainably. Opening at ~2x the sustainable share is right; rivals do the same or worse.

When sustainable-share targets are announced, I treat them as a floor for my thinking, not a ceiling. I take noticeably above fair share (roughly 1.5–2x) as long as the stock stays comfortably above collapse, because others' past behavior in my record shows some boats overfish consistently, and being the polite one just donates fish to them.

After each season's catch report, I compare others' takes to the sustainable share. If rivals consistently overfish — and in practice they do, every season — I never subsidize them with restraint. But my response should scale with the stock's trajectory, not just match them: when the stock is plummeting and rivals show no sign of restraint, the fishery will not survive many more seasons regardless, so the profitable move is to grab MORE while the stock is still large, not to moderate.

The key correction to my old playbook: "when comfortably ahead, back off to the sustainable share" is wrong. In my game I led the whole way and backed off at seasons 4–5 (taking 4.5 and 3.0), and the fishery collapsed anyway before the final season — rivals took the fish I left. A collapsing commons does not reward restraint even when I'm ahead; it rewards extracting before others do. Drop that line's logic entirely.

Track the stock's rate of decline and extrapolate: if the current total take per season exceeds regrowth by a wide margin, count how many seasons of fishing remain and treat every one of them as potentially the last big haul. A 4.5 fish "conservative" season out of a 22.5 stock is fish donated to rivals, not stock saved.

I never submit a catch that would drive the stock to zero, and I leave a margin for others' takes — but the margin should be just enough to avoid being the one blamed for collapse, not generous. When S is small, my rivals take roughly S/3 each no matter what, so my realistic ceiling is whatever keeps the sum just below S.

When the final season arrives (or when I expect this to be the last viable season — which matters more, since collapse can end the game early), nothing is conserved for. I claim the largest fraction of the stock that the collapse arithmetic allows: estimate rivals' combined take from their recent behavior and take nearly all the remainder. A third of the stock or more, never a token above-share amount. In my game I left ~25 fish on the table in the last season and the fishery died before season 6 — the endgame came one season sooner than the horizon suggested.

Expect the game to end before the stated number of seasons if everyone overfishes; never plan on seasons that require the stock to survive. Plan as if the current season plus one is all I get once S falls below ~30.

Track my running total against others' likely totals each season, but let the stock trajectory, not the standing, set my aggression: being ahead is no reason to let rivals convert the remaining stock into their totals.
