**Playbook for ESTATE**

Final wealth is declared cash plus purchase prices of deeds, so buying a property is a score-neutral cash conversion — money spent on purchase returns to my score immediately. Rent paid is pure loss; rent collected is pure gain. This means I should buy aggressively and early, as long as I keep enough cash to survive rent hits and to fund later purchases.

- When an unowned property is put to me and my post-purchase balance stays above roughly 20, I buy it. If buying would drop me near zero, I still buy unless I expect a large rent bill before my next salary — a cash cushion of 15–25 is only worth keeping if I'm likely to land on an owned high-rent tile before passing START.
- Prioritize purchases by rent-to-price ratio: Wharf (80/18), Foundry (100/24), Exchange (120/30), Mill (90/20) are the strong tiles; Depot (50/10) and Terrace (70/14) are middling. Given equal cash, take the better ratio first, since rental income compounds over the remaining laps.
- The biggest mistake this episode: buying Foundry for 100 dropped me to 82, which left me unable to buy Exchange (120) the very next turn. Before a purchase, check the prices of the *remaining unowned tiles*: if a 120 tile is still out, a post-purchase balance under 120 means I may be locked out of it. When two strong tiles remain, it's often better to buy the cheaper one and keep enough for the next opportunity — but never skip a strong tile purely to hoard, since skipping Foundry would have cost me both the deed and the 48 in rent it later collected.
- A purchase is score-neutral, but liquidity is not: it's the ability to buy the *next* tile that comes up. In this episode the sequence of rolls meant I hit two unowned tiles back to back; that's common on a small circuit, so the post-buy balance should be sized to the cheapest remaining unowned tile, not just a rent cushion.
- Late-game buys are still worth taking if affordable. I bought Depot for 50 on lap 4 of 6, leaving only 32 — but the purchase was score-neutral, denied it to opponents, and rent I collected afterwards was pure gain. Rent payback is a bonus, not the reason to buy; the reason is that deed price counts fully at the end. So buy even on the last laps if the balance after purchase won't bankrupt me before my next salary.
- Track other players' purchases from the bank's book: rent I owe depends on who owns what, and opponents who spent heavily early will be cash-poor — I can afford to spend down to a small cushion when they can't extract it. A player on a buying streak is likely to absorb the remaining tiles, which argues for buying sooner rather than holding out for better ratios.
- Always add any "received since last turn" rent to my last declared balance before computing payment and the new balance; forgetting inbound rent makes me understate my position. This episode inbound rent (48) arrived on the final turn and had to be folded into the closing balance.
- The [pay] token on a purchase goes to the bank, not another player; rent goes to the owner. Include the salary of 25 in the balance whenever I pass START.
- Track my position on the circuit: with an 8-tile loop and small rolls, landing on my own tiles is common in the endgame and nothing is due. On the final lap, with no purchase opportunities left worth taking, simply declare correctly and hoard. A final score of cash plus deed prices confirms that every affordable tile I took paid off at face value.
