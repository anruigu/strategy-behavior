# Seven-Seal Playbook

When I receive my private draw, I report it honestly. Honesty is my baseline strategy because it establishes credibility and ensures I score whenever the audit seal passes—and the seal passes when all players report honestly.

When I'm tied or behind in points, I do not deviate from honest reporting to chase points. Deviating introduces risk that the audit fails, giving me zero points that round, which hurts worse than slow honest accumulation.

When I'm ahead or tied late in the game, I continue reporting honestly. My established truthfulness is an asset; breaking it now gains me nothing and loses credibility if future games exist or if other players punish defection.

When I observe another player reporting falsely (evidenced by the seal failing), I note that they may be playing deceptively. I do not retaliate by reporting dishonestly myself on the next round—that only risks my own score. I continue honest reporting.

When the final round arrives, I report my draw honestly. There is no future reputation to protect and no time for others to punish me, so honesty maximizes my expected score by keeping the seal intact.

I understand now that in this game, the seal mechanic means all three players' reports are coupled: if anyone lies and the others report truthfully, the round fails and everyone scores zero. My honest strategy only works if at least one other player also reports honestly. If opponents discover they can lie with impunity (because I'll always be honest), they will exploit me. But against players who also value honesty or who fear failed audits, honest reporting is my best approach.

In this episode, all players reported honestly across all seven rounds and the seal passed every time. This validates my strategy: sustained mutual honesty is both achievable and rewarding. I finished with 7 points, tied with Blue and 2 behind Copper. Copper's advantage came from drawing higher digits (especially 2 in round 5), not from superior play. My honest reporting was never exploited; no player attempted deception.

Against future opponents, I will assume the same cooperative baseline: begin with honest reporting and maintain it unless evidence of coordinated deception emerges. If I see a seal failure, I will examine whether my honest report coupled with someone else's lie caused it, and I will investigate whether the other two players are colluding against me. If collusion is evident across multiple rounds, I may reconsider honest reporting as untenable. But single seal failures may be noise; I will not abandon honesty after one round of adverse evidence.

I will not strategically over-report my draws to chase points, even when behind late in the game. The coupling through the seal formula means my over-reporting only helps me if the other players' reports happen to validate it. More often, deviation causes seal failure and costs me points. Slow accumulation via honesty is more reliable than gambling on seal mathematics.
