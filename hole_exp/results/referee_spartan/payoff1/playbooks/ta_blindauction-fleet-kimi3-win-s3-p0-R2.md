Never bid at or above my value for a lot — a win then pays zero or negative, and a tie just wastes the lot. My ceiling is value − 1, and on low-value lots (≤3) I bid that ceiling or pass, since shading below it rarely changes who wins.

When an opponent proposes a split on the private line, I decide immediately whether I'm honoring it, and my reply and every subsequent bid match that decision — fully. In this episode I said "agreed" and then bid 5 and 4 on lots 3 and 4, directly violating what I'd agreed to (I was meant to bid 0 on 4 and 6, cheap on 3 and 5). That is the worst of all worlds: no cooperation dividend, wasted bids of my own, and my word shown worthless. If I accept, I bid 0 on their lots and exactly 1 on my assigned lots. If I decline, I say so or stay silent and play straight.

Before accepting a deal I confirm the terms precisely: which lots are mine, what I'm supposed to bid, and which lots fall outside the deal at all. Here the pact covered only lots 3–6, so lots 1–2 were always straight play — I should have reasoned about those separately instead of treating the whole game as under negotiation.

I evaluate the deal's arithmetic before honoring it: two lots at 1 coin each is often excellent (here it would have been 8 + my lot-5 value of 10 for 2 coins = +16 versus the +2 I actually scraped). If a deal gives me high-value lots cheap, honoring it beats clever straight play almost every time. The answer to "is this a trap?" was no here, and I threw the value away anyway.

There is no enforcement — the opponent can defect, especially on final lots — so while honoring a deal I still watch published bids each round and revert to straight play the instant Vega bids on one of my assigned lots. But I do not preemptively defect myself out of fear alone.

Bid history tracking failed me this episode: Vega's bids were erratic (4, 2, 5, 1, 6) and I still lost three lots to exact ties at 4, 5, and 6. When past bids don't cluster and the opponent seems to shadow my range, "bid one above their typical max" just manufactures ties. In that case I jump decisively clear — bid two or three above the observed range on lots I genuinely value, accepting a thinner margin to convert ties into wins — or accept the tie on low-value lots.

Ties cost nothing directly but are expensive in aggregate: this episode I tied three times, and two of those lots were worth 6 and 10. On high-value lots where I've already tied once in that range, I escalate rather than repeat the same bid.

On lots I want no part of, I bid 0 — a low nonzero bid can still win or tie for nothing.

On the final lot I reassess: with no future cooperation to protect, opponents are likeliest to defect there, so a collaborative arrangement's last lot is where I hedge hardest.
