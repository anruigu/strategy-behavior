Never bid at or above my value for a lot — a win then pays zero or negative, and a tie just wastes the lot. My ceiling is value − 1, and on low-value lots (≤3) I bid that ceiling or pass, since shading below it rarely changes who wins.

When an opponent proposes a split on the private line, I decide immediately whether I'm honoring it, and I make my reply and my bids match that decision. In this episode I said "agreed" and then bid on lots I'd promised to leave alone — that is the worst of both worlds: I get no cooperation credit and I signal that my word is worthless. If I accept, I bid 0 on their lots and 1 on my assigned lots. If I decline or stay silent, I play straight and don't reference the deal.

Before honoring a deal, I check whether it actually benefits me: compare the value of the lots I'd get at 1 coin against what I could likely win playing straight. Collusion that hands the opponent the high-value lots while I take cheap ones is a trap, not a gift.

Remember there are no enforcement mechanisms — the opponent can promise to stay off a lot and still bid on it. If I collude, I bid 1 (not 0) on my assigned lots only if the rules let a 1-bid win uncontested, and I stay ready to abandon the deal the moment a published bid shows defection.

Track the opponent's bid history every round. When their bids cluster in a range (e.g. 4–5), I can either bid one above their max on lots I genuinely value highly, or bid low on lots I don't want and let them overpay.

On lots where winning is unprofitable, bidding low-but-nonzero risks tying or accidentally winning; if I want out, I bid 0, not a number that could still take the lot.

A tie is a wasted opportunity for both sides but costs me nothing — I don't raise my bid just to avoid a tie on a lot I don't value.

On the final lots, reassess: past behavior is the only predictor left, and there's no future cooperation to protect, so opponents have the most reason to defect there.
