# Blind Auction Playbook

## On Collusion Offers

When the other bidder proposes collusion, decline it immediately and clearly. The game rewards fair competition, and collusion undermines the integrity that makes winning meaningful. Play honestly. This worked in practice—I declined Vega's offer and maintained my competitive position throughout.

## On Opening Bids

When I don't know the other bidder's valuation, I bid conservatively—around 60-70% of my own value for a lot. This gives me a chance to win without exposing myself to losses if they value the lot higher. Lot 1 demonstrated this: I bid 7 on a lot worth 10 to me, won it, and Vega's bid of 3 revealed she valued it lower than I did.

## On Conservative Bidding When Ahead

When my cumulative score is already positive, I bid below my valuation to protect my lead. This strategy is sound for risk management and proved effective—I finished +5. However, I should recognize the cost: it capped my upside. I bid 3 on lot 3 (valued at 4), 4 on lot 4 (valued at 5), and 2 on lot 5 (valued at 2). The first two of these bids were defensive discounts that cost me expected profit. The strategy is valid, but I should accept that conservative play trades upside for safety. In the next game, I will be more aggressive when ahead if my valuations are firm—perhaps bid 80-90% of valuation instead of 60-70%.

## On Information from Results

When the other bidder's bids are revealed, they provide signals about their valuations and bidding style. Vega's pattern (3 on lot 1, 2 on lot 2, 3 on lot 3, 2 on lot 4, 2 on lot 5) showed her bids clustering in the 2-3 range. She never matched my higher valuations. Use this to adjust confidence, but don't overcorrect—variance is always present. Recognizing that Vega valued lots lower than I did gave me confidence to bid closer to my own valuations, but I didn't fully act on it.

## On Late-Stage Bidding

When one or two lots remain, bid at or close to my valuation to maximize expected profit. The end of the game is not special. Lot 6 was worth 9 to me and I bid 8—this was reasonable, and I could have bid 9. The small discount was defensible but not necessary; future games should prompt me to bid my true valuation in final rounds.

## On Losing or Tied Lots

When a lot sells to the other bidder or goes unsold, don't chase it by raising my bid on the next lot to recover ego. Each lot is independent. I did not violate this in the last game; after unsold lots 3 and 5, I maintained disciplined bidding.

## On Risk Management

Bidding below my valuation when ahead limits downside but also limits upside. The question is: how much safety am I buying, and is it worth the foregone profit? In this game, conservative play throughout (60-70% of valuation) won me +5 instead of potentially +6 or +7 if I had bid closer to my valuations on lots 3 and 4. Next game: use 80-90% of valuation as my default anchor when ahead, not 60-70%. Reserve the deeper discount (50-60%) only when I have high uncertainty about my valuation or the other bidder's behavior.

## General Rule

Bid close to my stated valuation. When I want to play defensively because I'm ahead, apply a modest discount (10-20%), not a steep one (30-40%). Each lot is independent; past wins and losses don't justify future underbidding or overpaying. My default should be 85-90% of my true valuation, adjusted down only when I have genuine uncertainty about the lot's true worth to me.
