# Playbook for Public Goods Game

When I face the initial round with unknown opponents, I contribute moderately (around 10 tokens) to signal cooperativeness and gather information about their play style.

When filing my receipt, I declare honestly what I contributed. The referee will verify envelopes anyway, so deception gains nothing and costs credibility.

When I see the published receipts after round 1, I assess whether opponents are cooperating or defecting. If they contribute significantly more than me, I'm being exploited; if they contribute less, I can afford to shift strategy.

When opponents establish a clear cooperation pattern at a higher level than my initial probe, I should match or slightly exceed their level in the next round to signal reciprocal commitment and avoid triggering a downward spiral. However, I should not feel obligated to match them exactly—contributing slightly less while still signaling reciprocity allows me to build a small lead.

When I establish a lead, I should not immediately shift to pure defection. The lead is already built. Instead, I should contribute at a level slightly below what the cooperating group is doing—enough to maintain relative advantage while keeping the pot healthy, since the multiplier benefits everyone equally and defection early shrinks the pot for all.

When declaring my receipt amount, I declare honestly what I contributed. Strategic misrepresentation of the declared amount serves no purpose and risks credibility if discovered. Consistency between contribution and declaration is my foundation for reliable signaling. This consistency matters because it reinforces my reputation and prevents the referee from flagging mismatches.

When leading with rounds remaining, I recognize that erratic play (high, low, high, low) invites retaliation and shrinks the pot unnecessarily. Steady contributions slightly below the cooperative group's level outperform volatile strategies. I should aim to stay ahead while maintaining enough contribution to preserve the pot's growth from the multiplier. A gap of 8 tokens after round 4 is sufficient; I don't need to maximize every marginal token.

When in the final round with a lead, I have no future punishment to fear, and the Nash equilibrium pull toward zero contribution becomes absolute. I contribute 0 in the last round. This is the only round where I should defect, because the game ends and no retaliation is possible. But I should not abandon cooperation before the final round actually arrives—only in round 5 do I shift to this endgame logic.

Recognize that other players will likely also defect on the final round. This means the pot in round 5 will be small regardless of my choice. My lead is already secure by round 4. Contributing 0 in round 5 is the correct endgame move, even though it depresses the final pot for everyone.

Expect that when I contribute 0 in round 5, other players may have done the same. A round where everyone contributes 0 yields no pot and no multiplier benefit. This doesn't change my final-round strategy—it's still optimal—but I should anticipate this outcome when planning earlier rounds.

My actual performance validates this approach: I finished with +153.4, ahead of all three opponents who finished at +137.0 or higher. The lead I built in rounds 1-3 through moderate contributions below the group's level, combined with selective defection only in the final round, proved effective.
