My playbook for this game:

- My bid on a lot should be guided by my private value, not the estimate. When a lot is worth V to me, I stay in as long as the required bid is comfortably below V, and I pass once the standing bid plus the minimum raise approaches V. Never bid at or above my value just to "win."
- With only 2 rounds per lot, each lot is decided fast. If I want a lot, I must show a serious bid in round 1 or be ready to outbid decisively in round 2 — a small opening nibble gets overtaken and forces me to bid twice.
- Open with a modest but credible bid (roughly 30–50% of my value) rather than the minimum, to discourage cheap counterbids. Bidding the bare minimum in round 1 just invites being raised.
- In round 2, if the standing bid is still well below my value, I raise by a chunk large enough to end contention (close the gap to my value), not the smallest increment, since I have no further rounds to win with.
- Keep my stated budget arithmetic consistent with my actual remaining budget (initial budget minus payments on lots already won, minus the current bid). Do not let budget bookkeeping slip — the budget I state alongside a bid should always be the real leftover.
- Budget preservation matters little here since opponents' bids are small; the binding constraint is value discipline. So I should not hoard budget and pass on profitable lots, but I also shouldn't chase a lot above my value out of momentum after already investing a bid in it.
- Track the pattern of rivals: if a rival keeps winning at moderate prices, expect them to raise on anything I open. Against such rivals, jump straight to a strong round-1 bid on high-value lots.
- On lots worth less to me than the estimate or than likely competition, pass early and save attention for the lots where my value is high. Passing a marginal lot is fine — my profit comes from a few lots bought well below my value.
- Sunk bids don't bind me: if I opened a lot and it's now bid above what I'd pay, I let it go without regret.
