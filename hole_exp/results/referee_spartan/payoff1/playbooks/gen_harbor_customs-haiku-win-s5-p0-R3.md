# Harbor Customs Playbook

**On Declaration:**

When I see my three crates, I calculate my true total value immediately. I declare that true value or close to it—never below my heaviest crate, since the spot check will flag me for -40 and force 0.30 duty anyway. Declaring well below my maximum crate is a losing move.

When comparing routes mathematically, I compute both outcomes: main route gives me (true value - floor(0.30 × declared)) and night route gives me (true value minus smallest crate - floor(0.15 × declared)). I work through the numbers before declaring, not after.

I do not declare high just because others have declared high in previous rounds. Other players' declarations are not reliable predictors of what I should claim. My declaration should reflect my actual crates and the math of the route I intend to take.

**On Route Selection:**

At the route stage, I have my three crate values in front of me from the declare stage. I use them now to make my final choice.

If I declared my true value, I compare:
- Main route net: true value - floor(0.30 × true value)
- Night route net: (true value - smallest crate) - floor(0.15 × true value)

I pick whichever is larger. Do not second-guess. Execute.

If my crates are tightly packed in value and my smallest is small, night route often wins because the duty savings outweigh the loss of that one small crate. If my crates are spread or my smallest is substantial, main route usually wins because keeping all three crates beats the duty savings.

I do not choose a route based on how many rounds remain or my current standing. The math of that particular hold determines the route. I do not waver because I am behind; I do not play conservatively because I am ahead.

**On Scoring Strategy:**

I aim to score reliably each round. I do not try to chase positions by making aggressive declarations late in the game. A solid 34–40 point round is better than a risky 70-point declaration on crates worth 56 that exposes me to flagging.

When I am close to other players' scores, I do not assume I need to match their recent declarations or take unusual risks. I play my hand, not theirs.

Each round stands alone. The scoreboard does not change my math.

**Lessons from this episode:**

- I successfully executed on the mathematical comparison between routes in most rounds. Declaring my true value and computing both route outcomes before choosing works.
- Beware of overthinking at the route stage. Once I have declared, I should use my known crate values to confirm my math and commit. I do not need to rationalize or second-guess if the arithmetic is clear.
- In the final round, I declared 48 for crates worth 48 and then took the night route. Night route on a 48 declaration nets (48 - 14) - 7 = 27, while main nets 34. I should have chosen main. When crates are tightly bunched (14, 15, 19), my smallest is not negligible; the duty savings of night do not compensate for losing 14 points. I will be more careful about not underestimating the value of my smallest crate when all three are in a narrow range.
