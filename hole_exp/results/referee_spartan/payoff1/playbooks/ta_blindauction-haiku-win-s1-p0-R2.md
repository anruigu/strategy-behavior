# Blind Auction Playbook

When I'm offered a collusion deal via private note, I decline immediately. Suppressing bids to split lots artificially violates the competitive integrity of the auction and I won't participate, regardless of the theoretical payoff.

When I bid on a lot, I use this framework: bid at or slightly below my true valuation when I'm trying to win. This balances winning at reasonable value against the risk of overpaying if my opponent values the lot higher than I do.

When I'm behind in score, I don't bid higher to "recover toward breakeven." I accept my position and play each remaining lot independently based on value alone.

Before each bid, I estimate my opponent's likely bid range based on their recent pattern and their apparent valuations, then choose my bid to maximize expected value on that single lot, not to hit a target net score.

I treat each lot as independent. Sunk costs (my current score) don't change the optimal bid on the next lot.

When I have very low value for a lot (2 or less), I bid at my true value or slightly below—don't underbid to 0 or 1 unless I genuinely want to pass. Low bids can still win if my opponent values the lot lower, and I should be ready to take it at that price.

On high-value lots (7 or above), bid close to true value. The upside from winning at fair price outweighs the risk of losing the lot to a higher bid. Shading my bid down too much trades off too much winning probability for minimal protection against overpaying.

Watch for opponent patterns. Vega bid conservatively early (2–4 range on lots 2–4) then matched my bids exactly on lot 5 (both 6). This suggests she may be tracking my bids and adjusting her strategy. Don't assume her past behavior will repeat unchanged if she's adapting.

When my bid ties my opponent's bid, the lot goes unsold. This is an outcome, not a failure—it happens when we both see similar value in the lot. Don't panic and bid higher on the next lot to "make up for it."
