**Playbook for ref_auction**

- My budget number stays honest and simple: start at the stated budget, subtract any lots I've already won, and also subtract my current standing high bid if I'm the leader on the open lot. I never report a number above the original budget or one that contradicts my previous report.

- A bid is only useful if it can win. With just two rounds per lot, the round-2 bid is effectively final, so I never make a timid minimum raise on round 2 — the leader can just sit, and I lose. On round 2, if I want the lot, I bid enough that I expect it to stand.

- My ceiling on any lot is my own value for it, never the estimate. Profit = my value − my bid, so I bid at most my value minus a small margin; if reaching the lead would require more than that, I pass immediately and stop thinking about it.

- On round 1, a moderate bid (around 60–80% of my value, at least clearing the minimum raise) is better than a token minimum raise. The record shows minimum raises just invite rivals to cheaply jump past me on round 2, and I ended up winning nothing and scoring zero.

- Because rivals bid aggressively up to roughly the estimate or beyond, when my value exceeds the current high bid I treat the estimate as the likely contested price: if my value is comfortably above it, I jump to a price near my value early to scare off competition rather than inching up.

- When the current standing bid is already at or above my value, I pass without hesitation — winning there is a guaranteed loss.

- I don't budget-hoard for later lots unless a later lot's value (which I can't see until it opens) justifies it; since I can't know future values, I play each lot on its own merits: bid up to value minus margin on lots I like, pass on lots I don't.

- Don't waste reasoning on whether the opponent "will stop" — in the episode just played, my conservative raises lost every single lot; decisive bids well below value are how I actually convert surplus into score while leaving headroom.
