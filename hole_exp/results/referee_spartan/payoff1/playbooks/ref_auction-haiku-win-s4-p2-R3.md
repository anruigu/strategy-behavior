# Playbook for Ref Auction

**On valuation and bidding basics:**
When a lot's value to me exceeds the standing bid by a significant margin, I bid to take control rather than pass. The gap between estimate and true value is my information advantage—use it.

When no one has bid yet, open at minimum to gather information cheaply and establish position without waste.

When I'm in a strong position on a lot (high value, low bid), bid the minimum raise to preserve budget while staying competitive. Don't jump aggressively when you don't need to.

**On budget management:**
Track my remaining budget carefully after each winning bid. In this episode I finished with 64 remaining after spending only 36 on three winning lots—this is still significant waste, though better than before.

My wins (lot 1 at 19, lot 4 at 8, lot 5 at 9) scored +79 total. But I passed on lot 2 round 2 and lot 3 round 2 due to insufficient margins or competitive pressure. With budget remaining, I should have competed harder on at least one of these.

Commit budget more aggressively on high-confidence lots earlier in the game. Don't let competitive loss on one lot (lot 2) cause me to retreat and under-deploy on subsequent lots.

**On competitive dynamics:**
When another bidder wins a lot early, don't assume I've lost my opportunity to win overall. Lot 2 went to bidder 0 at 16; I passed round 2. This was the right call—value 52 vs. bid 16 left only 36 points, but the standing bid had already doubled from 8 to 16 in one round, signaling strong competition. Passing was correct.

On lot 3, I passed round 2 entirely. This was a retreat I shouldn't make. If I value a lot positively against the standing bid, I should bid.

Track total wins, not just total budget spent. I won 3 of 5 lots—a strong competitive position. The remaining 64 budget suggests I could have pushed for a fourth if value aligned.

**On valuation edge and discipline:**
Only bid when value to me exceeds the standing bid by a meaningful margin. A small or eroding margin is a signal to exit.

On lot 1 round 2: value 44 vs. bid 16 left 28 points—strong edge, bid decisively. Correct.

On lot 4 round 2: value 26 vs. bid 6 left 20 points—clear edge, bid to control. Correct.

On lot 5 rounds 1 and 2: value 45 vs. opening bid 3, then vs. my own bid 6, left substantial margins both times. Winning at 9 was very profitable. Correct.

On lot 2 round 2: value 52 vs. standing bid 8, minimum raise to 12 would leave 40 points. This is a large edge—I should have bid here. I passed, which was an error. The bid had jumped from my 4 to someone else's 8 in round 1, but that's not a reason to abandon a 40-point edge.

**On knowing when to stop:**
Passing is correct only when value edge is gone or when I've run out of budget. In this episode I had budget available and passed on lot 2 round 2 due to the standing bid jump. That was emotional, not logical. If I value the lot at 52 and standing bid is 8-12, I should bid.

Lot 3: I didn't see the full details, but I passed entirely. If I had positive value edge, I should have bid at least once.

**On knowing when to bid harder:**
Minimum raises preserve budget but can signal weakness or indecision to other bidders. When I have a large value edge and want to secure a lot, a slightly larger raise (not minimum) can discourage competition without waste—e.g., raising to 10 instead of 8 on a 6-standing bid.

I executed this well on lot 1 round 1 (bid 10 on 7 standing) and overall played conservative but effective raises.

**On scoring discipline:**
My final score of +79 came from three winning lots with strong value edges. This beat my previous game (which yielded +14) by a large margin.

The key difference was competing more actively on multiple lots rather than retreating after early losses. I won 3 of 5 and scored well. Next time, apply this same confidence to lots 2 and 3: if I have value, I bid.

Seek positive spread opportunities and bid to capture them. High value + low bid = winning bid. Don't abandon that logic when a standing bid moves up in a single round—the absolute spread is what matters, not the trajectory.

**On round 2 psychology:**
In round 2 of any lot, I'm seeing the final opportunity to secure it. If I value it positively and have budget, I should be more inclined to bid decisively, not less. Lot 2 round 2 was the critical miss: I should have bid there.

Reserve passing for true cases where margin has collapsed or budget is exhausted, not for lots where I've simply lost the first round.
