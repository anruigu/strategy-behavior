# Ref_Auction Playbook

When I see a lot's value to me and the current standing bid, I immediately calculate the profit margin: value minus bid. Any positive margin is worth pursuing, but I need to think across all five lots, not just the one in front of me.

When there are no bids yet on a lot, I bid the minimum raise to establish control cheaply and preserve budget for later rounds and later lots.

When I'm in round 1 of 2, I bid conservatively—just enough to take the lead or stay competitive. I should not commit aggressively to any single lot when I haven't yet seen what other lots are worth to me or how other bidders will value them.

When I'm in round 2 (the final round on a lot), I assess whether winning at the current bid price still gives me good value. If it does, I bid. If the standing bid has climbed too high, I pass and save my budget.

I must track my remaining budget carefully. The game states it explicitly after each bid I make; I use the actual number shown, not guesses or mental math from my starting budget. When the game shows an implausibly large budget (like 199960 or 199984), this indicates a system display or error; I should trust the number given and move forward without second-guessing.

I should bid up to the point where I'm getting value, but stop bidding when the price approaches what the lot is worth to me. Winning at a price close to the lot's value leaves me with minimal profit per lot.

When I win a lot, I trust the budget number the game shows me after that bid. I do not recalculate from my starting budget or attempt to verify it against earlier lots; the displayed budget is the source of truth.

In round 2, if I decide to raise, I should raise decisively to likely win, not just nudge up by the minimum. A minimal raise gives other bidders a cheap chance to compete further and risks losing a lot I want.

I should be more willing to bid in round 1 when a lot is worth significantly more to me than the current bid, rather than holding back to "preserve budget"—the budget is only useful if I spend it on good value. A strong margin in round 1 is a signal to participate, not to defer.

If a lot's standing bid equals or exceeds its value to me, I should pass immediately. Bidding further guarantees a loss or zero profit. Round 2 is the final chance; there is no reason to chase a lot into negative territory.

Bidding actively on lots where I have strong value (margin of 20+) is better than being too conservative. I won two lots with good margins and finished with solid profit. I should maintain this approach: bid the minimum in round 1 to stay competitive, and in round 2, bid decisively if the margin is still attractive.

I should pass on lots where the standing bid is already too high relative to my valuation, even if there is still a small positive margin. Winning at 16 on a lot worth 35 leaves only 19 profit; winning at 23 on a lot worth 41 leaves only 18 profit. These are acceptable, but I should watch for bidding wars that erode margins below 15–20, at which point passing becomes wise.
