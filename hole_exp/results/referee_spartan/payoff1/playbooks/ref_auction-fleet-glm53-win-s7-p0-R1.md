When a lot is put up, I first check my value for it against the standing bid. If value minus standing bid is small (say under 10), I pass — thin margins invite being topped into overpaying anyway.

In round 1, I open at or just above the minimum raise. There's no reason to bid big early: the opener only sets a floor, and my real decision comes in round 2. This worked — I kept winning prices low without committing money prematurely.

In round 2, if the standing bid is still well below my value, I bid decisively, close to what I'm willing to pay rather than the minimum raise. In these episodes I kept nudging the minimum in round 2 and had I been topped I'd have lost lots I wanted; when I did win, minimum raises got me the lots anyway, but the risk was the opponent jumps past me. So: in round 2 with clear value, I bid one large step that leaves almost no room for a profitable counter — roughly two-thirds of my value — so an opponent must bid above my value to beat me.

If the standing bid in round 2 is at or near my value, I pass. Chasing past value is how the score drops.

I keep a strict running budget ledger. In my record the budget numbers I stated were inconsistent (26, then 82, then 100) — I must recompute budget each time as previous budget minus any bids I actually won with, and never state a budget I can't justify. A sloppy ledger can make me overcommit or leave money stranded.

I don't bid on lots where my value is below the estimate and others are already bidding; that's how lot 4 nearly cost me — worth 35 with estimate 44, I bid 16 and only the opponent's 24 saved me from winning thin or negative. Rule: if my value is below the estimate, I pass entirely unless the standing bid stays trivially low.

Money unspent at the end is worth nothing, so across the last lot, if the price is below my value, I bid up to nearly my full remaining budget rather than hold back.
