Never bid at my value for a lot — a win pays zero. My preferred bid on contested lots is value − 1; on low-value lots (≤3) I bid that ceiling or 0.

When an opponent proposes a split on the private line, I decide before replying whether I will honor it, and every subsequent action matches that decision completely. This episode I again said "agreed" and then bid 5 on lot 3 (a lot I'd promised to concede) — sabotaging a deal that would have given me lots 4 and 6 for 1 coin each. I left value on the table and made my word worthless. If I accept, I bid 0 on their lots and exactly 1 on mine. If I won't honor it, I decline or stay silent and play straight — never accept-then-defect without a new reason.

Before accepting a deal I check the arithmetic: which lots are mine, what they're worth to me, and what the split costs me. Here the deal gave me two lots for 2 coins total; had I honored it I could have picked up my assigned lots essentially free. Deals like this usually beat clever straight play, so I accept and honor unless the assigned lots are worthless to me.

Deals have no enforcement — the opponent can defect, especially on later lots. While honoring a deal I watch published bids each round and revert to straight play the moment they bid on one of my assigned lots. But I do not preemptively defect out of fear alone.

The pact in this episode covered only lots 3–6; lots 1–2 were always straight play. I must be clear about which lots a deal covers and reason about the rest separately.

On contested lots I track the opponent's bids: if they cluster in a band, I bid one above their typical max on lots I want; if their bids are erratic or shadow mine, repeating my range manufactures ties, so I jump decisively clear (two or three above the observed band) on genuinely valuable lots, accepting thinner margin to convert ties into wins. This episode I lost twice to a single point (lot 4: my 1 vs their 5) and tied once (lot 5: 5–5) — close misses are as costly as losses.

Ties cost nothing directly but waste the lot entirely; on a high-value lot where I've already tied at a number, I escalate rather than rebid the same number.

Vega often bids 4–5 even on lots I value modestly. Bidding 4–5 myself invites ties or losses by one; on lots worth 6+ to me, 6 or 7 is usually the right competitive bid.

On lots I want no part of, I bid 0 — a low nonzero bid can still win and cost me, or tie.

On the final lot, no future cooperation remains to protect, so opponents are most likely to defect there; on the final lot of any arrangement I hedge by bidding my straight-play value rather than the deal bid.
