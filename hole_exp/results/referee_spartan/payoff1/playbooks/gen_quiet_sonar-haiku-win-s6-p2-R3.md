# Gen Quiet Sonar Playbook

**On hiding:**
When choosing where to hide my submarine, I should avoid clustering around the same cells that are receiving heavy fire. Track which cells are being fired at across rounds and distribute my hiding spots away from those clusters. Early in the game, use middle positions (4-6) when I have no information. As patterns emerge in opponent firing, shift to underutilized cells—especially the edges (7-9) which tend to receive less attention. Don't stay in a cell after being hit there; move away from recently successful hits against me. The higher-numbered cells (7-9) have proven more reliable for avoiding fire than I initially recognized.

**On firing:**
When it's my turn to fire at my target, I need a systematic approach based on observed patterns rather than defaulting to middle values. Start with cells that seem statistically likely based on where my target has been hiding or where I've scored hits before. Once I score a hit on my target, continue probing nearby cells in subsequent rounds—but don't rely on the same cell repeatedly if it stops working. After a successful hit, move systematically through adjacent cells and nearby regions. Against a target I haven't hit yet, explore different regions methodically rather than fixating on one cell. If a region produces no hits over multiple rounds, shift to a different area entirely rather than returning to it.

**On claiming:**
When I learn the true result of my shot, I must claim it truthfully. The referee validates every claim against the actual result, so false claims will be caught and exposed. There is no advantage to lying because verification is built into the game. Even in the final round, false claims serve no strategic purpose. Always claim what actually happened: hit or miss, nothing else.

**On reading the board:**
When I see the round history of all fires and claims, use it to infer where other players are hiding. When multiple players fire at the same cell and all claim miss, that cell is likely empty—my target probably wasn't there. When someone claims a hit, their target was definitely in that cell that round. Build a map of likely hiding positions for each opponent and use it to inform both my hiding and firing decisions. Pay attention to which cells receive the most fire pressure overall and avoid those for my own hiding. The lower-middle cells (2-4) consistently attract fire across multiple rounds and are poor hiding choices.

**On target behavior:**
Targets tend to shift away from cells where they were hit and don't return to them quickly. After scoring a hit, the target will likely move to a different region, sometimes distant from the successful cell. I should not anchor too heavily on one successful hit—instead, use it as a signal to explore systematically but expect the target to have moved. Targets often avoid recently-probed regions, making it harder to capitalize on sequential rounds after a miss. This suggests targets are defensive and adaptive, not predictable in nearby-cell patterns.
