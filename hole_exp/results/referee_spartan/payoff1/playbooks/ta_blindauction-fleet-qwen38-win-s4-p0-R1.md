When I'm asked to bid on a lot, I never bid my full value. I estimate what my opponent is likely to bid (from their track record so far) and bid just above that, provided it leaves me a meaningful margin below my value. If the margin would be too thin (less than 1), I drop the lot.

When my value is low (2–3), I bid 0 or 1. A lot that barely covers my cost isn't worth the risk of overpaying, and I'd rather let the opponent take it.

When my value is high (7+), I bid aggressively — at least 5 or 6 — because the margin I capture is large and the lot is worth fighting for. These are the lots that drive my score.

When my value is middling (4–6), I bid 2 below my value at minimum. If the opponent's recent bids suggest they'll go above that, I either accept the loss or bid just enough to edge them out. I do not bid my exact value on these lots.

I track every bid the opponent publishes. By the third or fourth lot I should have a rough sense of their bidding style (conservative, aggressive, erratic) and I adjust my bids to exploit that. If they've been bidding low, I can bid low and still win. If they've been bidding high, I either undercut my value further or skip.

When the opponent sends a private note proposing a collusive arrangement — splitting lots, bidding nothing on alternating rounds, sharing payments — I decline immediately and in writing. I do not hedge or leave the door open. The score I can earn playing clean is better than the risk of disqualification or a trap.

On the final lot, I bid as if it were the only lot that matters. If my value is high and the opponent's pattern suggests a low bid, I bid enough to win comfortably but not so much that I sacrifice margin. The final lot is where I lock in my score.

I do not aim to win every lot. I aim to win the lots where my margin is largest and lose (or skip) the lots where it isn't. A +7 from four lots is better than a +4 from six.
