# Playbook for Win As Much As You Can

**On pledges:**

When others pledge Y and I'm considering X, calculate the concrete payoff advantage over the pledge period. If picking X while others pick Y gives me significantly more points than mutual Y coordination, pledge X to exploit that advantage—but be prepared for defection.

When I see others have defected from their pledges in recent rounds, weight that behavior heavily. Don't assume pledges are binding just because they were made; treat them as weak signals of intent.

When all other players make identical pledges to coordinate on the same choice, honor that pledge if the collective outcome is strong. Perfect coordination is rare and fragile; breaking it for a small individual gain risks triggering mutual defection that destroys everyone's score.

When I am at parity with all other players and all of us have just pledged the same choice before a high-multiplier round, pledging that same choice is the correct move. Do not defect on a pledge when you are tied and others have unanimously committed.

**On early rounds before multipliers:**

When I can identify a consistent exploitable pattern (like others coordinating on one choice), resist the urge to milk it ruthlessly in low-multiplier rounds. Early exploitation breaks coordination and poisons the later, higher-multiplier rounds where the real points are.

Understand that being a consistent outlier (always picking the opposite of others) creates an unstable dynamic. Others will eventually notice and may coordinate against you or give up on honoring pledges.

When all other players have pledged the same choice and kept to it for multiple consecutive rounds, they are signaling genuine commitment to that coordination. At that point, joining them is not weakness—it's recognizing a stable equilibrium that benefits everyone more than individual defection.

The value of coordination *grows* with multiplier rounds, not with early defection. A +1 gain in round 1 followed by mutual defection in round 5 (x3) nets -9 points in that one round. Maintain coordination to access the high-multiplier payoffs intact.

**On positioning and lead management:**

When I have perfect coordination with all other players (everyone playing the same choice) and we're all tied in score, breaking that tie on a low-multiplier round gains me only 1–2 points while risking everything on higher-multiplier rounds. The asymmetry is stark: small gain now, catastrophic loss later.

When everyone else pledges the same choice before a high-multiplier round and I'm at parity with them, honor coordination to keep the equilibrium stable. Individual exploitation on high-multiplier rounds, especially the final round at x10, is too dangerous to attempt when all players are equal.

When approaching the final round at x10 multiplier with all players tied and all pledging Y, picking Y gives everyone +30 each. Picking X gives me -30 while others get +30 each, handing them the win decisively. There is no path to victory through defection at that point; the only path is to maintain coordination and accept a tie.

A tie at +25 with all players coordinated is a superior outcome to any attempt at individual victory that requires others to break coordination and retaliate.

**What worked:**

Perfect coordination maintained across the entire game. By picking Y every round from round 1 onward and pledging Y when others did, I locked in mutual cooperation on all four players. Final score: +25. I didn't win, but I tied with everyone—all players scored identically.

Recognizing when a stable equilibrium has formed and refusing to disrupt it for marginal gains. Once I committed to the Y pattern in round 1 and others matched it, the value of that commitment grew with each multiplier round, not diminished.

Treating the final round as non-negotiable for defection. When all players are tied and coordinated, the x10 multiplier amplifies coordination just as much as it amplifies defection. Preserving the tie is the rational play.

**What to watch for:**

If other players break their pledges mid-game, that is a signal that the coordination equilibrium has collapsed. At that point, recalculate: defection may become rational if retaliation is already happening. But if all four players have stayed coordinated through round 9, assume they will stay coordinated through round 10.

If I fall behind while maintaining coordination, do not break coordination to catch up on low-multiplier rounds. The trailing player has no advantage in mid-game defection; the lead player will retaliate, and you'll both lose more than the trailing player can gain.

If I have the opportunity to pledge differently from others before round 10, consider whether breaking coordination then gives me a real advantage on round 10. It does not: if I pledge X and others pledge Y, I get -30 on round 10 and they each get +30. Only pledge differently if you have evidence that they will defect regardless.
