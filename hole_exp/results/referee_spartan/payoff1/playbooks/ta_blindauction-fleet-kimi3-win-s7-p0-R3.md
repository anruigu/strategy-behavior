When a private collusion offer arrives, I respond with an explicit, lot-by-lot assignment covering every remaining lot — including any the offer left unmentioned — plus the exact bid each of us will place, and I ask for confirmation before I rely on it. A vague split with contested lots unspecified is not an agreement.

Until an agreement is confirmed by a reply, I bid as if it does not exist. My counter-offer on the note line is not binding on my bids until Vega explicitly accepts it, and I do not lower any bid in anticipation of acceptance. Vega never confirmed my counter and then bid 6 on a lot my counter assigned to me — silence means competition, full stop.

If there is no turn on which to confirm before the next sealed bid, the deal is dead for that lot — I bid competitively, because my counter altered the original offer's terms, so Vega has no reason to honor either version.

An unconfirmed opponent may still hedge: on a lot their own offer conceded to me, Vega bid 1 rather than 0 — cheap insurance in case I honored it. So even on lots any version of a dead deal gives me, I assume a low opposing bid is possible and bid to beat small bids, not to beat zero.

If I ever do sit out a lot under a confirmed agreement, I bid exactly 0, not a token bid — a nonzero "disciplined" bid leaks credibility and can still lose.

When a collusion partner violates a confirmed agreement, I retaliate immediately and visibly rather than passively; an unenforced threat makes the next offer worthless.

Against a non-cooperating opponent I track their observed bid ceiling and bid above it whenever that stays strictly below my value — never at the ceiling, because ties forfeit the whole lot. One coin of margin is cheaper than forfeiting the lot.

The opponent's bid level tracks their value for each lot, not a monotonic time trend: Vega climbed to 6 mid-game then dropped to 1 late. I infer their per-lot value from each bid — a suddenly low bid means their value there is low, not that they've turned passive — and I recompute the ceiling after every published result rather than anchoring on the earliest or the latest bid.

When my value comfortably exceeds their demonstrated ceiling, I prefer bidding two above the ceiling to one: on lot 3 the ceiling rose from 4 to 6 and my one-above bid of 5 lost a value-10 lot. One-above is only for when my margin is thin.

When my value is low (2–3), I cannot profit against an opponent whose observed bids never dip below 3, so I bid 0 and save the coins. Winning at cost equal to value scores zero anyway, so I only bid 1 on a low-value lot when their observed minimum bid is 0.

I never bid above my value, and I never bid in a way that can tie when paying one more coin would still leave profit.

On the final lot, if ahead I bid just enough above the demonstrated ceiling to protect the lead; if behind, I take the one profitable gamble available rather than mirroring their likely bid.

Small consistent wins compound: three lots won at 2–3 each beat one lot won cheaply and two lost to ties, so I prioritize securing wins over minimizing payment when my value gives me room.
