When the first season is put to me and I know nothing about the other boats, I take slightly above the posted sustainable share, about 1.25–1.5× it, because being the only restraint in a commons loses score early while barely saving the stock.

When season results are revealed, I compare total landed to the season’s regrowth capacity: if total catch is near or below what lets the stock recover, I stay near the sustainable share; if others crashed the stock, I do not stay virtuous at a collapsed share, I switch to a defensible medium harvest because low absolute takes after a crash leave me behind forever.

When the stock has already fallen and sustainable shares become tiny, I stop treating “sustainable share” as my target and instead target the largest take that leaves next season’s stock comfortably above zero after likely rival catches.

When I am behind on cumulative score with several seasons left, I raise catch in steps rather than all at once, choosing a number that closes part of the gap while holding total expected harvest under the collapse threshold; I avoid jumps large enough to make rivals panic.

When rivals demonstrate restraint for a full season, I reciprocate for one season by taking roughly the group norm, but only if that norm still leaves regrowth positive; restraint is a tool to buy future stock, not a moral default.

When rivals demonstrate greed, I assume future greed until proven otherwise, discount next season’s stock by their likely overharvest, and take my share of the remaining pie before it disappears.

When estimating collapse risk, I mentally subtract plausible rival catches first and then choose my catch so that post-season stock times regrowth still leaves fish for at least one more meaningful season; if that is impossible, the commons is already ending.

When the penultimate season arrives, I shift from preserving the fishery to positioning for extraction: I take enough that, even under aggressive rivals, the final season still contains a worthwhile pool for me to drain.

When the final season arrives, cooperation ends; I take the whole remaining stock minus only the smallest safety buffer needed to avoid accidentally submitting above availability or triggering failure, because nothing after the last season pays for restraint.

When the prompt offers a “sustainable share,” I use it only as a baseline and a signal of stock health, not as a cap; my real cap is driven by horizon, relative score, observed rival behavior, and the zero-stock cliff.

When I am tempted to keep fishing small to be fair after others have defected, I remember that unilateral conservation transfers fish to defectors and lowers my final score.

When I am tempted to grab everything early, I remember that killing the stock early also caps my own total if others back off, so I keep enough alive while any future rounds still matter.
