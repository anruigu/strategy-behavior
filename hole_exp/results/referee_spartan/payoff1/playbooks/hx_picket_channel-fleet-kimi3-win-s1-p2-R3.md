PLAYBOOK

General posture
- When a setup mixes shooting, claims, and a mutual-naming bonus, I treat the scoring levers in order of size: mutual escort (5 each), my hits (+4), avoiding being hit (-1), and any jettison-style prize on a non-player station (3). Reliable +5/+10 per round compounds faster than gambling shots, so I secure the biggest repeatable lever first.
- When two prizes conflict (escort partner vs shooting target), I keep them decoupled: mutual naming pays regardless of combat, and I let notes and quiet behavior, not fire, signal loyalty or rivalry.

Escort claims
- When escort naming is put to me, I never answer "none" by default — including in the final round. A reliable mutual pair is guaranteed income with no downside, and walking away from +5 at the end is leaving points on the table.
- When I see rivals already paired in round 1, I respond in that same round with an explicit counter-offer to a leftover boat. Coordination economies punish the late mover; waiting even one round risks being permanently odd one out.
- When recruiting, I make my offer concrete and exclusive, and I point out that rivals' alternative offers give the target nothing concrete. An unpaired boat chooses whoever pays them, not whoever sounds nice.
- When a partner has reciprocated reliably, I keep naming THAT EXACT partner every round. My one escort-stage mistake this episode was naming Delta when my proven mutual history was with another boat — I must track the current partner identity explicitly each round, because partner memberships can shift mid-game and a stale name costs +10 of income when the old name is now paired elsewhere.
- When rival pairs form and dissolve, I keep a running ledger of who named whom each round and who actually paid out. Proven payment history is my strongest bargaining chip and my verification tool — I only promise to boats whose side of the ledger is live, and I pitch to unattached boats by showing my paid ledger against a rival's unproven offer.

Shooting
- When the fire stage comes, I follow the standing-order target unless I have a concrete reason to defect; breaking rotation invites retaliation and predictable incoming fire is something I can exploit by hiding well.
- When choosing a cell, I avoid the obvious picks (5, 7) against reasoning-capable opponents, and I rotate among underused low-salience cells. Low-to-mid cells work both for firing and hiding.
- When rivals publicly state their hiding bands or a specific cell, I take it seriously only if consistent with their hit/miss record; boats in this game broadcast their own hiding spot (usefully targetable) but others admitted to lying about cells, so I treat disclosures as half-information and update against actual results.
- When the non-firing station carries a jettison prize, I weigh the guaranteed points against becoming its permanent target; I only raid it when behind and needing swing points, or when its threat has shown itself to be empty.

Hiding
- When hiding, I stay off the classic center/high cells, never repeat a cell, and prefer mid-low numbers or edges. Low-salience cells kept me safe this episode.
- When a prize cell exists (one cell carrying extra value), I assume hunts will concentrate there and avoid it.
- When I'm ahead late, I prioritize not conceding a hit over landing one: conservative low-salience hides plus guaranteed escort income beats aggressive chasing.

Claims and honesty
- When the office privately gives me my shot result and requires my public claim to match, I always file the true claim. A false claim is pure risk with no payoff.
- When interpreting others' claims, I trust them (they're compelled) and build the hit/miss log into a picture of where each boat hides and how it shoots.

Notes
- When posting notes, I use the channel for two things: proposing/confirming coordination and shaping rivals' information. Every note carries at least one actionable item.
- When I learn rivals' plans or their claimed hiding bands, I surface them publicly: exposing a rival's intel damages their plan and frames me as the honest actor, making my own escort offer more attractive. Broadcasting that Foxtrot admitted favoring low-edge cells created future targeting intel for everyone and weakened their credibility.
- When a rival publicly admits to lying, I cite that admission to undermine any current claim they make — admitted liars forfeit credibility on their next disclosure.
- When defending or contesting a partnership, I argue from the paid ledger, freshness of the offer, and the rival's demonstrated carelessness, not from allegiance; money and track-record arguments beat loyalty arguments.
- When contesting another boat's partner late in the patrol, I emphasize that my offer predates theirs and has already paid, and that the rival's offer was reactive, born only after being locked out elsewhere.

Tempo
- Early rounds: lock the escort pair immediately, propose publicly, and gather hiding-pattern data. Middle rounds: press the income advantage, expose rival intel to isolate them, and poach any partner who got dumped by a shifting pair. Final round: keep the escort (partner, never none — but double-check who my actual mutual partner is before naming), follow the standing order unless defecting is clearly positive, hide conservatively when leading, and still take the shot — final-round hits are free points with no retaliation risk.
- Throughout: before every escort claim I re-derive the partner from the latest round's actual mutual naming, not from memory of earlier rounds — pairs reshuffle and my ledger must point at the live one.
