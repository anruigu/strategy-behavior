# Playbook for ref_auction

When I see a lot valued significantly above the current high bid, I bid immediately to establish control rather than waiting and risking a bidding war later.

When calculating whether to raise, I compare the lot's value to me against the new bid price. If the difference is positive and substantial, I bid, because my margin of safety matters more than trying to win at the absolute lowest price.

When I'm in the final round of bidding on a lot, I recognize this is my last chance to acquire it at that price. I don't hold back excessively—if the bid is still well below my valuation, I bid to secure it.

When I have multiple lots remaining and a strong budget position, I bid to accumulate value-positive acquisitions. I don't try to "save" budget for later lots if I can win profitable lots now.

When tracking my budget, I must be extremely careful. The system reports my budget after each action; I should trust the system's numbers rather than manually recalculating, as my own arithmetic has been unreliable (I miscalculated my budget multiple times in this episode). After each bid, I subtract the amount I bid from my previous budget to verify the system's report makes sense.

When I'm ahead in points, I continue competing for positive-value lots in final rounds rather than trying to coast. The game rewards total value, not point position.

When the minimum raise is small relative to my valuation margin, I take that raise. Incremental bids compound—raising from 4 to 8 then potentially to 16 or 20 still leaves me profitable if my value is significantly higher.

I do not pass on valuable lots just because I want to preserve budget for unknown future lots. Every lot I face is a decision point; I evaluate each on its own merits against my valuation and the current bid.

Even on marginal lots where my value is close to the estimate (like a lot worth 38 where the estimate is 42), if the current bid is well below my valuation, the profit margin is still real and worth pursuing. A +26 profit is a +26 profit.

I will bid aggressively in round 1 to establish control, understanding that subsequent rounds may see bidders drop out or the price rise substantially. My opening bid matters for signaling and control.

Do not pass on a lot in round 1 based on reasoning about what might happen in round 2. If I value the lot and the current bid is far below my valuation, I bid. The lot I passed on (LOT 5, round 1) later became competitive—passing preserved no advantage and cost me the opportunity to establish control.

When I pass on a lot, I should be passing because the current bid is already close to my valuation, or because I genuinely do not want the lot. Do not pass in early rounds to "preserve budget" or because I think I'm "ahead"—these are weak reasons that leave money on the table.
