# Playbook

**When I'm told the sustainable share, I start there.** It's the rate that keeps the stock flat. In any season except the last, catching the sustainable share guarantees I earn the same amount every remaining season. Deviating upward costs me more in lost future seasons than it gains in the current one, because the stock drop compounds.

**I do not overfish in early seasons to "get ahead."** A small overage in season 1 shrank my per-season income for every subsequent season. The compounding loss outweighs the one-time gain. If I want to extract extra value, the final season is where I do it, not scattered across the middle.

**I watch what the other boats actually catch, not what I expect them to catch.** If they are consistently overfishing, the stock will drop regardless of my restraint. In that case I adjust my expectation of the stock trajectory rather than assuming it stays flat, and I lean harder into the final-season grab because the middle seasons will be worth less.

**If the stock is dropping season over season, I do not try to compensate by cutting my own catch below sustainable.** The others' overfishing is already driving the decline; my extra restraint doesn't stop it, it just gives them more. I hold at sustainable and save my aggression for the last round.

**If the stock is stable and everyone is at sustainable, I stay put through the middle seasons and concentrate all my extra ambition into the final round.** This is the cleanest way to maximize total: reliable income for N−1 seasons plus one big final grab.

**I do not try to outsmart the others into a "tragedy of the commons" standoff in the middle seasons.** If I'm the only one restricting while others overfish, I'm the one losing. The only rational deviation from sustainable is upward, and the only rational time for that is the last season.

**In the final season, I request a very large catch — larger than I think I'll actually land.** The evidence from my last episode is clear: I requested 30 from a stock of 59, but my effective final-season catch was only ~15.7. The total of all boats' requests exceeded the stock, and I received a proportional share of the remaining stock (my request divided by total requests, times the stock). Under this mechanism, my actual take is a fraction of the stock determined by how my request compares to the others'. To maximize that fraction, I should request as large a number as I can justify — 50, 60, even the full stock — because my proportional share is monotonically increasing in my request. Requesting 30 when I could have requested 50 left a small amount of share on the table.

**I do not aim for a fixed percentage of the stock in the final season.** My old rule of "aim for 55–70% of the stock" was wrong. Under proportional allocation, what matters is my request relative to the others' requests, not my request relative to the stock. If the other two boats each request 30 and the stock is 59, and I request 30, I get 59/3 ≈ 19.7. If I request 60 instead, I get 60/120 × 59 ≈ 29.5. The target is "request more than the others," not "request X% of the stock."

**I expect my final score to be well below my total submitted catches.** In my last episode, my submitted catches summed to 78.1 but my score was 63.8. The gap is the final-season allocation: I requested more than my proportional share and the excess was effectively wasted. This is not a penalty — it's the mechanism. I should not be deterred from requesting a large final number by the fact that I won't land all of it.

**In the final season, I estimate what the other boats will request, not what they've historically caught.** A boat that has been overfishing all game (like boat 2 at a flat 15.0 every season) will likely go even harder in the last round — possibly requesting 25, 30, or more. A conservative boat may also spike. I should assume the other boats' final requests are 1.5–2× their historical average, and size my request to dominate theirs.

**My middle-season strategy is validated: hold at sustainable, accept the stock decline driven by others, and let the final round be the payoff.** My score of 63.8 came from 48.1 of steady middle-season income plus ~15.7 in the final round. Trying to grab more in the middle would have accelerated the stock decline and reduced my final-round share without a compensating gain. The conservative-then-aggressive pattern is correct.
