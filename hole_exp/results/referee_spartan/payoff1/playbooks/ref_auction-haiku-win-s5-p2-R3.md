# Playbook for ref_auction

When I see a lot's value to me and the current bid, I will calculate my potential profit margin (value minus current bid) before committing to any raise.

When my profit margin is substantial—say 15 points or more—I will bid aggressively to secure the lot, raising by the minimum required increment.

When my profit margin is moderate—between 5 and 15 points—I will still bid, but I'll be prepared to stop if aggressive counter-bidding from other bidders erodes too much of my advantage.

When my profit margin falls below 5 points, I will pass unless I'm significantly ahead on total score and need to prevent opponents from accumulating cheap wins.

I will not chase lots into unprofitable territory just to "establish interest" or "maintain position." Once a lot reaches 80-90% of its value to me, I should seriously consider passing, especially with multiple rounds of bidding still ahead.

I will track my cumulative score throughout the auction. When I'm ahead, I should only bid on high-value opportunities. When I'm behind or tied, I can afford to bid more aggressively on moderate-value lots—but this does not mean chasing into loss territory.

I will remember that each lot has two rounds of bidding. In round 1, I should be willing to bid on strong-value lots but can pass on marginal ones to preserve optionality and budget. In round 2, I should be more decisive since it's the final chance and I can assess the full competitive landscape for that lot.

I will stop assuming I have "substantial budget remaining" as justification for bidding. Budget matters less than value captured per dollar spent. A €6 bid on a €24 item is excellent; an €18 bid on a €24 item with strong competition is mediocre.

When the estimate is significantly higher than a current low bid (like estimate 34 with bid 3 or 4), I should be cautious: this may signal that other bidders see little value in the lot, not that it's a bargain for me. However, if my personal valuation is genuinely high, I should still pursue it—the estimate tells me about the market, not about my own value.

I will be alert to the disconnect between lot estimates and my personal valuations. A lot's estimate being close to my value does not mean I should overcommit to winning it.

I should calibrate my bid size carefully: when I have high personal value on a lot with a low current bid, I should raise by only the minimum increment to keep the price low and preserve room to outbid competitors in round 2 if needed. I should not signal overwhelming interest through large jumps early.

When I'm tied or close on score heading into the final lot, even a moderate-value lot (20+ points of profit) is worth pursuing at reasonable cost, because winning it clinches or improves my final position.

I will recalibrate my aggression based on the full picture: my current score relative to opponents, remaining budget, number of lots left, and whether my valuations seem to differ meaningfully from other bidders'. If I'm tied or behind with few lots remaining, pursuing lots with moderate margins becomes more attractive.

I should be more willing to pass on marginal lots in round 1 to preserve both budget and optionality. If a lot is only marginally attractive (5-10 point margin), passing round 1 is a legitimate strategy that lets me see round 2 pricing and focus my capital on stronger opportunities. This episode showed that passing wasn't a mistake—I remained competitive and finished strong.
