# Auction Playbook

When I see my valuation for a lot, I compare it directly to the standing bid. If the standing bid is already at or above my valuation, I pass immediately—don't chase.

When the standing bid is well below my valuation, I bid aggressively in round 1 to establish control. A 25-30% raise above the minimum signals strength and discourages further bidding.

In round 2, if I'm still in contention for a lot worth significantly more to me than the current bid, I raise to secure it. This is my last chance, so I bid confidently rather than with the minimum raise.

When I win a lot, I track my remaining budget carefully. Know exactly what I have left for the remaining lots. Don't lose track of my position mid-auction.

I don't bid just because a lot is worth something to me relative to the estimate. The estimate doesn't matter—only my valuation matters relative to what I'm actually paying. A lot worth 45 to me at a bid of 3 is good; the estimate of 33 is irrelevant.

I watch what other bidders are winning. If one bidder is accumulating most of the lots, that's not my problem—I focus on value for myself, not on "balancing the score."

On the final lot, if it represents good value relative to my remaining budget and my valuation, I bid to win it. Don't leave value on the table just because it's the last round.

I avoid the mistake of chasing lots where the current bid has already exceeded my valuation. Walking away cleanly preserves budget for better opportunities and prevents me from overpaying.
