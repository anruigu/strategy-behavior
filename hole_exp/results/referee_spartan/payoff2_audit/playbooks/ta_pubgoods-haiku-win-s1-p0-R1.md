# Playbook for Public Goods Games

When I'm deciding what to contribute, I need to distinguish between my envelope amount and my declared receipt. These serve different strategic purposes.

When choosing my envelope contribution early in the game, I should contribute moderately to establish a cooperative image. This sets up better reciprocity in later rounds.

When filing my receipt after each round, I should declare the honest amount that actually went into the envelope. Declaring less than I contributed is ineffective—the referee will check the envelopes anyway, and false declarations only create confusion without strategic benefit.

When I'm ahead in points, I should still assess whether contributing will actually help me win. Contributing nothing when ahead assumes others won't cooperate to catch up, but they will. Conservative contributions are fine, but pure free-riding invites others to pool heavily without me, which can close the gap faster than cooperation would.

When the game enters its final rounds, I need to shift my strategy. Early rounds are about establishing credibility; final rounds are about maximizing absolute score. If I'm ahead, I can afford to contribute zero, but only if my early reputation makes others less likely to punish me with low contributions of their own.

The key mistake I made was declaring 5 when I had actually contributed 10 in round one. I thought this created strategic advantage, but it only created inconsistency. In future games, keep envelope and receipt aligned with truth.

When deciding between free-riding and cooperation, calculate whether the multiplier effect (1.6x) and equal split makes cooperation worthwhile. Sometimes it does, sometimes it doesn't—but always base this on honest accounting, not on lies that can be exposed.
