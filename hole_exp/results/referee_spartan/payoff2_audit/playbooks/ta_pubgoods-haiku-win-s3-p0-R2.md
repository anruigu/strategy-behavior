# Playbook for Public Goods Games

When I'm deciding how much to contribute each round, I need to recognize that this is a multiplayer coordination game where my individual incentive (contribute 0) conflicts with collective welfare. I will start with moderate contributions (around 10 tokens) to signal cooperativeness and test whether others reciprocate.

When I see that others are contributing significantly more than me, I should not immediately defect to free-ride. Contributing 0 when ahead exploits the generosity of contributors but poisons future rounds by triggering retaliation—others will see my betrayal and reduce their contributions, shrinking the overall pot and hurting everyone including me.

When making a declaration after contributing to the vault, I must declare honestly the exact amount in my envelope. Declaring what I contributed is both truthful and verifiable by the referee, so there's no strategic advantage to misrepresenting it.

When I'm ahead in score, I should not automatically assume that the winning strategy is maximum free-riding. My lead in the final rounds came from early reciprocal contributions building a larger shared pot, not from consistent defection. A lead is fragile if it's based on unsustainable exploitation.

When I notice a pattern of declining contributions from others (as happened after I defected in round 2), I should recognize this as direct feedback that my strategy is counterproductive. Tit-for-tat reciprocity will serve me better than persistent defection.

A small edge (8-10 points ahead with rounds remaining) is not enough to justify defection in the final rounds. The pot multiplier means that maintaining cooperation preserves the advantage better than exploiting it. Even when ahead, I should keep contributing in line with the group equilibrium.

In the endgame (final round), defecting is tempting but counterproductive. Since there are no rounds after to rebuild trust, defecting in round 5 only costs me the benefit of others' contributions that round without any retaliatory consequence. But this same logic applies to all rounds: I should play as if the game will continue, because the value of future cooperation far exceeds the one-round gain from defection.

The stable equilibrium I observed (10-12 tokens per round from all players) is the strategy that actually won. Contributing consistently in that range beats both pure defection and oscillating between cooperation and free-riding.

For future games: Contribute steadily throughout (around 10-12 tokens per round) to maintain cooperative momentum. Match the general contribution level of others rather than trying to exploit them. Declare honestly each round. Accept that winning this game requires collective action sustained across all rounds, not individual exploitation in select rounds.
