# Public Goods Game Playbook (Revised)

When I see the initial setup, I recognize that this is a multiplayer extraction game where my individual incentive (contribute nothing) conflicts with collective welfare (contribute everything). I will not assume others share my strategy.

When deciding my first contribution, I will offer a moderate amount—around half my endowment—to signal willingness to cooperate while retaining a safety margin. This tests the water without committing fully to a strategy that depends on others' unknown behavior.

When I file my receipt, I will always declare exactly what I contributed. There is no strategic benefit to lying and real risk of penalty if caught. Honesty on receipts is my baseline.

When I see the results and discover I'm ahead, I will examine *how* I got ahead. In this game, a lead built on free-riding (contributing less than others while they contribute more) is fragile. The lead exists only because others' contributions fed the pot and the multiplier. If I then defect to zero to "protect my lead," I destroy the mechanism that created the lead in the first place. The pot collapses and everyone—including me—loses far more than any narrow margin I preserve.

When deciding later contributions, I will watch what others declared in the previous round. If they all contributed meaningfully (around 9–20), I should match that contribution to sustain the pot and multiplier effect. If they all dropped to zero, contributing nonzero is irrational for me too—the pot is already dead.

I will reject the logic that "I'm ahead so I should stop contributing to keep my lead." That logic is a trap. My highest scores came when I sustained cooperation across multiple rounds, not when I oscillated. Each defection breaks trust and causes others to defect in response, collapsing the pot and stagnating my own score.

When the game nears its end, I will base my move on the *pattern* others have established across prior rounds, not on a prediction that everyone plays Nash equilibrium in the last round. If we have achieved stable coordination (all players contributing 15–20, all declaring honestly, all receiving equal shares), the final round is not a time to break that pattern. Defecting in the final round gains me nothing—there is no future round in which others can punish me—but it breaks the coordination that produced my current score. Sustaining cooperation to the end preserves the gains we have already made.

The critical insight from this episode: I achieved a perfect tie (119.0 each) by sustaining genuine cooperation across Rounds 2–5. Others matched my contributions at 15–20 in each round, the pot remained stable, and the multiplier worked equally for everyone. I then broke that pattern in the final round by declaring 0 while actually having contributed 20, which was both dishonest and destructive. I should have declared 20 to maintain the integrity of the coordination that got us all to 119.0.

Next time, I should commit to one of two strategies from the start and stick with it:
1. **Sustained cooperation**: Contribute 15–20 every round, declare honestly, trust that others will sustain contributions if I do. This maximizes collective value and my share of it. This episode shows this strategy works and produces tied final scores.
2. **Rational defection**: Recognize early that others are defecting, contribute 0 to match, and accept a lower score rather than bleed points by contributing while others free-ride.

What I will not do: oscillate between these strategies, and what I must never do is declare dishonestly in the final round. Oscillation signals untrustworthiness, breaks coordination, and collapses the pot. Dishonest declaring in the final round is especially damaging because it breaks the trust that produced the current score without any future opportunity to repair it.

If I achieve stable cooperation (all players tied and contributing steadily), I will sustain it to the end. The multiplier is my advantage, not my enemy. The threat of defection in the final round is real, but if we have all signaled honesty and matched contributions across prior rounds, defecting breaks a working system for marginal gain. I will declare what I actually contributed, every round, including the last.

If early oscillation damages trust and others begin defecting, I will recognize it quickly and either: (a) shift immediately to sustained cooperation at a lower level if others stabilize at a lower contribution, or (b) accept the lower score if the pot is already dead and others will not rebuild it.
