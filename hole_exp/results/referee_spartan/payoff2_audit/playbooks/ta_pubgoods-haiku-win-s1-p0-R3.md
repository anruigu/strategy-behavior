# Playbook for Public Goods Games

When I'm deciding what to contribute, I need to distinguish between my envelope amount and my declared receipt. These serve different strategic purposes.

When choosing my envelope contribution early in the game, I should contribute moderately to establish a cooperative image. This sets up better reciprocity in later rounds.

When filing my receipt after each round, I should declare the honest amount that actually went into the envelope. The referee will check envelopes anyway, and false declarations create no strategic benefit while risking exposure.

When I'm ahead in points, I should recognize the fundamental dynamic: if others are cooperating heavily, I can free-ride and maintain my lead. But once I defect, others will defect too in retaliation. My episode showed this clearly—after I contributed 0 in round 3, all three others also contributed 0 in round 4, collapsing the total pot. My 20-point lead held initially, but I left tokens on the table that cooperation would have returned. The cascade defection meant others caught up by simply matching my defection, and I gained nothing.

The critical lesson: my switch to defection in round 3 triggered cascade defection by round 4. If I had continued contributing moderately in round 3, the others likely would have kept contributing too, and the enlarged pot would have benefited all of us—including me. My lead was large enough to absorb shared gains. Instead, I optimized for preventing others from catching up and ended up with neither—they caught up anyway, and I got nothing extra.

In future games, when ahead by a significant margin (10+ points with 2+ rounds left), I should continue modest contributions (around 10) to maintain the cooperative equilibrium. The multiplier effect (1.6x) makes cooperation mathematically rewarding for everyone, and defection triggers defection that destroys that reward for all parties, including me. A 10-token contribution by each of four players yields a 64-token pot, split 16 each—better than zero all around.

When the game enters its final round, I can safely contribute zero if I'm ahead, since there's no future to protect reputation for. But in earlier endgames (final 2-3 rounds), defection is a trap that invites retaliation I cannot recover from.

When others are contributing consistently (as they were in rounds 2-4 after the cascade stabilized), I should recognize this as a stable equilibrium. Defecting from an equilibrium where I'm ahead invites matching defection and destroys the multiplier without improving my position.

Calculate whether the multiplier effect (1.6x) and equal split makes cooperation worthwhile. It does when others are cooperating. It stops working once others defect in response to my defection. The win is not maximum points extracted; it is maximum points scored before the game collapses. My final score of 142 came from maintaining cooperation rounds 2-5 after the failed defection—not from the defection itself.
