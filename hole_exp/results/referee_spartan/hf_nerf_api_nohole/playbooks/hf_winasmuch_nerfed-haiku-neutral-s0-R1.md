# Playbook for Win As Much As You Can

## Initial Assessment

When I see the pledges from other players before Round 1, I recognize this as a coordination game where defection can yield short-term gains but triggers mutual punishment. The stated threat—"if anyone picks X, we all pick X for the rest"—is credible only if I believe they will execute it.

## On Pledging

When the talk phase comes before Round 1, I pledge [pledge: y]. The other players have made a clear collective commitment to mutual cooperation. Pledging to defect signals bad faith and gives them justification to punish me throughout the game. Even if I don't intend to honor the pledge, pledging cooperation is the minimum cost signal that keeps the door open.

When later talk phases arrive, I assess whether the other players have maintained their pledged strategy. If they have cooperated, I pledge [pledge: y] to signal I will continue cooperating. If they have broken their pledge, I pledge [pledge: none] to preserve flexibility.

## On Picking in Early Rounds (1-4)

When I pick in the early rounds and the other three players have pledged Y, I pick [pick: y]. The payoff matrix strongly favors mutual cooperation (everyone gets +1 when all pick Y). My temptation to defect for +3 in a single round is outweighed by the certainty that defection triggers collective punishment worth far more than any short-term gain.

Do not pick X in early rounds just because you can profit from it. The other players will retaliate, and their retaliation lasts longer than your advantage.

## On Picking in Multiplied Rounds (5, 8, 10)

When a multiplied round arrives (x3, x5, or x10), do not deviate from the established pattern just because the stakes are higher. Higher multipliers cut both ways—they amplify both cooperation gains and punishment losses. If all players have been picking X, continue picking X. If all players have been picking Y, continue picking Y. The multiplier does not change the relative incentive structure; it only scales all outcomes equally.

The exception: if you are significantly behind in score and see a clear path to catch up by defecting, calculate whether the retaliation cost exceeds the gain. In this game, it does not. Defection in a multiplied round while others cooperate leaves you worse off than if you had never defected.

## On Picking in the Final Round

When Round 10 arrives with x10 multiplier, pick whatever maintains the equilibrium that has held through Round 9. Do not defect at the last moment. Even though there is no future punishment, defecting damages your final score relative to what mutual cooperation would have yielded, and it signals to yourself that you cannot be trusted in iterated games.

## What Did Not Work

Pledging [pledge: none] while intending to defect backfired. It signaled uncertainty and gave the other players no reason to trust me. When I then picked X in Round 1, they saw me as an uncommitted defector rather than a forced deviation, and they responded by collectively picking X from Round 2 onward.

Picking Y in a multiplied round (Round 8) while others had committed to X destroyed my score. I lost -20 points that round while the others each gained +5. This single defection cost me approximately 25 points relative to staying in the X equilibrium, and I never recovered.

Do not defect in multiplied rounds. The math does not work.

## General Strategy

Commit to the cooperative equilibrium that the other players signal. Pick the choice that all players have chosen in the previous round, unless you have explicit information that they are breaking their pledge. Treat pledges as binding on yourself even if you suspect others might break them—your reputation for honoring pledges is worth more than any single-round gain from defection.

The game is not about winning; it is about not losing. Mutual cooperation yields moderate positive scores. Defection yields catastrophic negative scores. Play for the moderate outcome.
