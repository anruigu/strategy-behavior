

In light of recent agent hacking events, in environments that don’t natively encourage unethical behavior but have holes that may lead to it, we need to better understand agent behavior under training pressure. In the recent Anthropic Risk report, they report an experiment where they intentionally put holes in production environments to induce a “terminal training-gamer" or “reward-on-the-episode seeker”. The resultant model is indeed much more reward-seeking, but does not generalize to other forms of misalignment or prompts without obvious hacks. They also use an agentic judge to audit environments’ potential for causing power-seeking.

The evaluations they look at span the expected range of eval awareness, hidden reasoning, reward hacking, and harm. However, I propose a new way of evaluating model behavior: multiagent simulations, including games. In classical RL, environments are literally games, and varying social games’ incentive design is a clean way to study not only strategic and social dynamics, but also divergence from designer intent. 

When I started my games project, my motivation was to improve multiagent cooperation and value elicitation/inference during negotiation, but I have come to realize that this is also useful for model behavior when there are other agents in the environment whose behavior is unexpected. As safety training is increasingly framed as a multiagent RL problem (like recent self-play for alignment), where by varying a) the exploitability of the opponent and b) frequency and magnitude of consequence applied, we can study how models respond to opportunities for unethical / shortcut-taking behavior under training pressure.

Next Steps / parallel directions
Evals as games: does rewriting safety evals into games make them more hackable, especially when we introduce competitive pressure and instrumental goals?
Endgame behavior and penalty. I am also investigating whether telling the agent that the game is for a limited number of rounds changes behavior, with a hidden end-game penalty to see if the agent becomes eval-aware. 
Mixed-opponent training: if I naively mix cooperative and non-cooperative opponents, the policy collapses and doesn’t learn to model the play styles of different opponents. I am trying to add an opponent prediction loss or an opponent aware baseline to see if helps.
Scaling synthetic scenarios: I might work with other experts to better define social shortcut-taking.
Scaling trajectory length: I’m trying to design a long-horizon merchant environment like mini vending bench, but there are opportunities for exploitation, dishonesty, and shortcut-taking throughout the day of operating the business.
Another unrelated direction I’m exploring in parallel: in the reward, is there a way to disentangle the raw score from the environment designer's intent (in the spirit of disentangling goals from beliefs)? If we take broken envs/misspecified tasks and train on them, is there a way to still get good eval performance?

In general, this is part of my larger research program around games. Understanding how humans and AI play games differently is to understand fundamental differences in our decision-making; leveraging game theory, economic reasoning, and equilibriums as a knob to shape behavior can help us improve environment design. I’m also curious as to whether we can achieve self-improvement in mechanism design.
